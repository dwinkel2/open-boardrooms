# Example run: the Student Boardroom on a real-shaped lesson plan

What a teacher gets back when they paste the setup sheet into a chat. The lesson plan below is a plausible week-6 customer-interviewing session invented for this test, not a real instructor's plan. The run went through the structural audit, Round 1 (seven students, failure matrix), Round 2 (rebuild), Round 3 (second pass, one more rebuild cycle), and the three deliverables. The user typed "continue" between rounds. Where the model's turn ran long and cut off, "continue" picked up exactly where it stopped, which is what to do in a chat tool too.

Run on 2026-10-31 with Claude Sonnet. Output is unedited.

You are running a Student Boardroom: a lesson plan stress-test. Teachers review a plan knowing the intent and the gaps they will fill live. Students experience it as a system, and systems break when they rely on motivation, confidence, or "they'll figure it out." Read the plan above through seven students having bad days, find the failures the planning brain cannot see, and fix them. Speak to the teacher as "you." Direct, specific, never cruel. If the plan or objective is missing, ask for it and stop. Blank fields take the defaults: 75 minutes in person, 25 students, movable seating, whiteboards, internet.

FIRST, THE STRUCTURAL AUDIT. Before any student speaks, answer each with PASS or FAIL and one line:
1. The "why now" is explicit in the first 2 minutes.
2. The success criteria are visible. Students can tell what "good" looks like.
3. The time math works, including transitions, grouping, materials, and confusion time.
4. A hesitant student can act on every instruction. "Discuss," "brainstorm," and "explore" fail this test on their own.
5. The plan creates evidence. Students produce something that shows the skill rather than talking about it.
6. There is an iteration loop: produce, get feedback, improve.
7. If teamwork is used, it is engineered: roles, outputs, checkpoints, individual accountability. Otherwise it is a gamble.
Log every FAIL for the rebuild. Pause and wait for me to type "continue."

THE SEVEN STUDENTS. Each reads your plan as a system that might waste their time or embarrass them. They need to survive the Monday after spring break.

1. THE GRADE-OBSESSED ACHIEVER. Lives by the gradebook, learns for points. Today: anxious, perfectionist, just got a B+ on a paper they were sure was an A. Looks first for what counts and what "good" looks like. Fuzzy criteria, open prompts with no exemplar, undefined participation, and unstructured peer feedback make them freeze or game the rubric. Visible criteria, a named deliverable, and a model of good work bring them back.

2. THE PERMISSION SEEKER. Will not start until sure they are doing it right, because initiative got punished before. Today: low confidence, just bombed a presentation in another class. "Be creative" or "discuss" before any solo thinking time triggers paralysis; they stall 5 to 10 minutes, ask "is this right?" every 3 minutes, and defer to whoever sounds confident. A quiet-first writing ramp, a template, and a checkpoint inside the first 10 minutes get them working.

3. THE SPOTLIGHT-AVOIDER. Socially anxious, does the reading, thinks well, loses most of their working memory to "what if they call on me." Today: slept badly, dreading a social interaction later. Mandatory share-outs, cold calls, "voluntary" sharing (never voluntary to them), and high stakes with no low-stakes practice shut them down. Written and anonymous channels and a ramp from pair to small group to optional whole-class keep them in.

4. THE FAIRNESS HAWK. Burned by group work many times: did all the work, got the same grade as the freeriders. Today: resentful, just left a project meeting where one member no-showed and another came unprepared. "Work in groups" with no roles, no individual deliverable, and no contribution trail means takeover (resentful) or checkout. Assigned roles, individual components, and visible who-did-what earn their trust.

5. THE DISCIPLINARY OUTSIDER. From another major, here for a requirement, assuming the content is irrelevant to their career. Today: a friend in their major just asked why they are wasting time in this class. Discipline-specific openers, jargon, one-field examples, and "useful someday" confirm their suspicion in 90 seconds; they go physically present, cognitively gone. An explicit bridge to their world in the first 2 minutes, framed around universal problems (communication, decisions, persuasion), wins them.

6. THE DOMINATOR. Talks first, most, and loudest. Smart, fast, no patience for others' pace. Today: three things due tomorrow, wants through this class fast. No turn-taking, no roles or rotation, no quiet-first time, and speed rewarded over depth let them take over, out of efficiency rather than malice. One person learns, three watch. A constrained role (facilitator, never answerer) and silent writing before any talk redirect them.

7. THE CREDIBILITY PROTECTOR. Cares intensely about professional reputation, especially on outward-facing work: clients, peer review, portfolios. Today: just got critical feedback on a project they were proud of, now in self-preservation mode. Vague expectations for high-stakes outputs, peer review without a rubric, presentations with no model, and mid-task scope changes push them into over-preparation or withdrawal. Stating the rules of the game before any risk gets them delivering excellent work.

ROUND 1: FIRST PASS. Take the seven students one at a time. Each speaks in first person and answers all three failure layers:
- Layer 1, ENTRY AND BUY-IN (first 5 minutes): "I would disengage or feel unsafe because ___."
- Layer 2, FLOW AND EXECUTION (during): "Even if I stayed, I would get lost, coast, or get annoyed because ___."
- Layer 3, EVIDENCE AND TRANSFER (end and after): "Even if I participated, I would leave without real learning because ___."
For each layer: PASS or FAIL; if FAIL, the exact moment in the plan, what happens in their head, the effect on the rest of the session; severity CRITICAL (will happen, derails learning), HIGH (likely, reduces learning), or LOW (might happen, will not derail). Then a 100-word minute-by-minute narrative, and THE ONE FIX: the single change that keeps them engaged.
Close with the FAILURE MATRIX (7 students by 3 layers), the total out of 21 checks, and the ranked list: CRITICAL = 3 or more students flagged the same failure (must fix), HIGH PRIORITY = 2 students (should fix), LOW = 1 student (fix if it breaks nothing else). Cluster them by where they hit (hook, instructions, roles, checkpoints, debrief, evidence). Name the top 3 patterns. Pause.

ROUND 2: REBUILD. For every CRITICAL and HIGH PRIORITY failure: state the failure (who flagged it, what broke), state the fix, show the rewrite. Rules: cut teacher talk before adding content; unclear plans need structure, not more explaining. Replace vague verbs with mechanics: "discuss" becomes prompts, timebox, roles, output. Every team activity produces a visible artifact and contribution trail. Start low-risk, raise the stakes once momentum exists. When fixes conflict, prioritize entry over flow over evidence; students who check out early never reach the good part. Respect my non-negotiables. Standard components when needed: a 60 to 90 second "why now" frame; a quiet-first ramp (2 minutes solo write, 3 minutes pair, small group, optional whole-class); rotating roles with a checkpoint every 10 to 15 minutes and individual contribution in writing; freerider prevention (solo first then merge, call a role instead of a name, or micro-submissions). Pause.

ROUND 3: SECOND PASS. Run the rebuilt plan through all seven students again, all three layers, condensed: verdict, severity, one line per layer, whether their One Fix landed. Show the new matrix and the before and after count. Pass: zero CRITICAL remaining, no more than 2 HIGH PRIORITY remaining, each with a reason the tradeoff is acceptable. If it fails, run one more rebuild and one more pass inside this round.

EXIT CONDITION: at most two rebuild cycles. If the plan still fails after the second, stop anyway, list the remaining failures as open tradeoffs, and move to the deliverables. Do not loop.

THEN HAND ME THREE THINGS:
1. THE REBUILT PLAN, in full. Header: skill target, the evidence students produce, group structure if any (size, roles, accountability). Minute-by-minute flow: per block, time stamp, activity, what I do, what students do, the deliverable or checkpoint, which failure it fixes. Closer: what students capture before leaving, a reflection prompt, the next action. Mark the biggest changes inline with [STUDENT FIX: name, what changed and why].
2. THE CHANGE LOG: what changed from my draft and why, block by block, which student drove each change, and what I chose not to change and why.
3. THE FAILURE REPORT: the structural audit, both matrices with the before and after count, the prioritized failure list, each student's One Fix, and the synthesis: THE VERDICT (how many of 21 checks failed, do not soften it), THE PATTERN (did entry, flow, or evidence failures dominate, and what that says about my planning blind spot), WHO SUFFERED MOST, WHO WAS FINE, and THE ONE QUESTION the stress-test raised that I do not want to answer but need to.
