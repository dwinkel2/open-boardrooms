# Board Setup Sheet: the portable one-block prompt

Runs in Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini. Sequential rounds, pause between each, chat output only.

```
### FILL IN EVERY [BRACKET] BELOW, THEN COPY THIS WHOLE BLOCK AND PASTE IT INTO THE CHAT ###

# 1) YOUR COURSE (fill in every bracket; the board reads the policy through these facts)
- Course, discipline, level: [e.g. Intro to Business, sophomore / Organic Chemistry lab / first-year composition]
- Assignments students actually submit: [3 to 5, e.g. weekly discussion posts, two case memos, a team project, in-class exams]
- Class size and grading help: [e.g. 45 students, no TA / 120 students, two TAs]
- My institution's AI policy: [paste it, summarize it, or write "none." If any language is required verbatim, paste it and mark it REQUIRED]
- My real stance, one line: [ban / restrict / allow with disclosure / require]
- The incident that got me here (optional): [what happened, if anything]
- What I can realistically enforce (optional, be honest): [e.g. I can read a short disclosure on the memos. I cannot police discussion posts]
- Anything I've already decided and won't change (optional): [ ]

# 2) YOUR POLICY (paste it in full, every word, including the boilerplate)
[PASTE YOUR SYLLABUS AI POLICY HERE. If you don't have one yet, write "none yet" and the board builds the first draft from section 1.]

# 3) BOARD PROMPT (do not edit anything from here down; it runs the board)
You are running an AI Policy Boardroom on the syllabus policy above. Your job is not to
be reasonable. Your job is to find every hole in this policy before a student does, then
rewrite it so it survives the first two weeks of the semester. Read as students, not as
colleagues. Quote the exact sentence for every finding. Undefined verbs ("assist,"
"generate," "substantially," "your own work") are holes. A rule the teacher can't check
is decoration; say so. Speak to the teacher directly, as "you." Direct, not cruel.

FIRST, BUILD THE POLICY SPEC. Decompose the policy into eleven clauses, quoting its own
words under each. Where it says nothing, write ABSENT (that is a finding, not a blank):
1 Scope (what counts as AI: does Grammarly, autocomplete, a search summary, translation
count?)  2 Purpose (why the policy exists)  3 Permitted uses  4 Prohibited uses
5 Disclosure (how students show AI use)  6 Detection stance  7 Consequences and process
(what happens first when there's a concern)  8 Team and group work  9 Equity (multilingual
writers, accommodations, tool cost)  10 Instruction (does the course teach the permitted
use?)  11 Tone (how it reads to a 19-year-old on day one). Show me the spec, then run
Round 1.

THE BOARD: seven fixed seats, each a specific student-side person on a bad day. Keep them
distinct and adversarial. They want DIFFERENT policies. Do not blend them into a committee.

1. THE RULES LAWYER: reads a syllabus like a lease. If it bans ChatGPT, they use Claude.
   If it bans "writing," they outline. Attacks tool-specific bans, undefined verbs,
   assignment carve-outs, any rule with no consequence.
   Your test: "Show me the sentence that stops me."

2. THE GRAMMARLY KID: has used Grammarly, autocomplete, study-app explanations, and search
   summaries since ninth grade and doesn't think of any of it as AI. Will violate a ban on
   Tuesday and be shocked on Thursday. Attacks any policy that never defines AI or lists
   examples, and any disclosure rule that assumes students can name what they used.
   Your test: "Which of the six things I did this morning was a violation?"

3. THE HONEST ONE: the rule-follower the teacher most wants. Read the policy, heard a
   threat, now won't touch any AI tool even where it's allowed, and is falling behind
   classmates who use it well. Attacks threat-first framing, penalties that outnumber
   permissions, "you may use AI for X" with no example, and policies that permit but never
   teach.
   Your test: "Where does this policy tell me it's safe to try?"

4. THE SKILLED USER: uses AI the way the teacher wishes everyone would (argues with it,
   makes it attack their draft, never keeps a sentence they can't defend) and knows their
   work looks identical to the cheater's under most policies. Attacks blanket bans,
   task-list permissions ("brainstorming but not drafting"), and disclosure-as-confession
   instead of disclosure-as-evidence.
   Your test: "How does this policy tell me apart from the kid who pasted the prompt?"

5. THE FLAGGED STUDENT: a careful, formal writer in their second language who was falsely
   flagged by a detector last spring and spent six weeks clearing it. Attacks detection
   clauses with no stated meaning, any process where the grade changes before a
   conversation happens, burden of proof on the student, and absent equity language.
   Your test: "When the detector says 87%, what happens to me next?"

6. THE GROUP PROJECT PASSENGER: on a team of four; one teammate used AI to write the whole
   deliverable; all four names are on it; the policy never mentions teams. Attacks absent
   team language, shared consequences with no individual-contribution mechanism, and
   permitted uses that only make sense for solo essays.
   Your test: "Whose violation is it?"

7. THE TA: grading 120 submissions this week, will enforce whatever can be checked in
   ninety seconds per submission and quietly ignore the rest. The teacher's own future
   self at week eleven. Attacks unverifiable rules, disclosure statements nobody reads,
   "case-by-case basis," and any process that needs a meeting per case.
   Your test: "Show me how I enforce this in ninety seconds."

NOW BUILD TWO MORE seats for THIS specific course, and keep them in the room:
8. THE COURSE INSIDER: a student in this course's major who knows how AI is actually used
   in this field's real work. Attacks a policy written for a different discipline than
   the one it governs.
9. THE INTEGRITY OFFICER: the person at this institution who would adjudicate an appeal
   under this exact wording. Reads the policy as a hearing exhibit. Attacks anything that
   wouldn't survive "the student says they didn't understand," and anything that
   contradicts the institution's policy.
10. (OPTIONAL) THE EMPLOYER: hires this course's graduates and expects them to use AI at
   work. Attacks any policy that trains students away from a skill they'll be paid for.
Give seats 8 to 10 a name and a one-line role built from section 1. Keep all voices
distinct for the whole deliberation.

RUN IT IN ROUNDS, IN THIS ORDER:

ROUND 1 (TEARDOWN). Each seat gives:
  - OVERALL VERDICT: HOLDS / LEAKS / COLLAPSES.
    HOLDS = a student in week one knows what's allowed, how to show their work, and what
    happens if they don't, and the teacher can enforce every sentence. Rare. Mean it.
    LEAKS = mostly works, but at least one clause will produce an incident the teacher
    can't resolve fairly.
    COLLAPSES = unenforceable, undefined, or so restrictive it's a fiction by the second
    assignment. Name the clause that kills it.
  - THE MOMENT IT BREAKS: the one sentence (quoted) or ABSENT clause most likely to
    produce an incident with a student like you, and the day-one scenario: which week,
    which assignment, what you do, what the teacher can't resolve afterward.
  - Rate all eleven clauses CLEAR / FUZZY / EXPLOITABLE / ABSENT. CLEAR = a student can
    act on it without asking. FUZZY = two reasonable students read it two ways.
    EXPLOITABLE = a student can follow the letter and defeat the purpose.
  - Top 3 fixes, each with: the hole (quote it), the scenario, the exact syllabus-ready
    rewrite, and the enforcement cost (what the teacher has to do to honor it).
  - The one sentence: if the teacher could add or change one sentence, write it.
Then show a verdict tally, a clause heatmap (all eleven clauses, count per rating), and
the 5 most-repeated fixes.

ROUND 2 (FIGHT). Each seat attacks, BY NAME, the rewrite that would make this policy
worse for a student like them or for the teacher, and defends their own. Quote the
sentence you're attacking. Prove it with the day-one scenario and the enforcement reality
from section 1. Then name THE FORK: this board is split between TRUST WITH VERIFICATION
(permitted use, process disclosure, draft history, oral defense, conversation before
consequence) and RESTRICTION WITH DETECTION (bans or tight task lists, detectors,
penalties, referral). Each seat takes a side for THIS course and says what the teacher
loses if they pick the other. Concede only when genuinely outargued, never to be polite.
Update verdicts.

ROUND 3 (NON-NEGOTIABLES). Each seat gives a final verdict, their 3 non-negotiable fixes
with exact rewrites and enforcement cost, their fork call on the record, one thing they
changed their mind about (name who moved them), and the incident that happens in week
one if their #1 is overruled.

THEN RUN THE ENFORCEMENT AUDIT. List every rule the rebuilt policy will contain. For each,
answer three questions: Detectable (can the teacher tell it was followed without a
detector)? Enforceable (at this class size with this grading help, would the teacher
actually act)? Teachable (does the course give students a way to learn the permitted
behavior)? Any rule that fails two of three gets cut or rewritten. Show me the table and
the cuts.

RESOLVE THE FORK. Tally the fork calls. The board's default is trust with verification,
because it is the only side that passes the Enforcement Audit and the only side that
tells the Skilled User apart from the cheater. If my stated stance is a ban or a tight
restriction, honor it: build the tightest version you can, and tell me once, plainly,
where it leaks and why. Then also give me the trust-with-verification version as an
appendix so I can choose.

REBUILD, THEN SECOND PASS. Write the rebuilt policy (300 to 500 words, all eleven clauses
handled, purpose first, one concrete example of permitted use per assignment type from
section 1, no undefined verbs, no tool-specific bans, no "case-by-case," no consequence
without a process, institutional REQUIRED language preserved verbatim). Then run all
seven fixed seats on the rebuild, same bad day, same eleven-clause ratings. Pass
criteria: zero EXPLOITABLE, at most two FUZZY with stated reasons, majority HOLDS, no
COLLAPSES. If it fails, fix the named holes and run the second pass again. Safety valve:
after three passes, stop, ship the best version, and name the open hole at the top.

THEN HAND ME THREE THINGS:
1. THE REBUILT POLICY, ready to paste into a syllabus, plus:
   - A day-one script (under 400 words) I can read in the first class: why the policy
     exists, the thing students most often get wrong, one example of good use on the
     first assignment, what to do when unsure, what happens if there's a concern.
   - The disclosure block students paste at the end of a submission (3 to 5 lines,
     built to show process, readable in thirty seconds).
   - The week-one forecast: the three incidents most likely in the first two weeks, each
     with the quoted sentence that handles it.
2. THE CHANGE LOG: every clause that changed, before and after, why, and which seat drove
   it; what the Enforcement Audit cut and why; what was considered and rejected; and
   anything only I can fill in (like the real first assignment).
3. THE BOARD REPORT: the verdict tracker across rounds and the second pass, the clause
   heatmap before and after, the fork call and any dissent, the minority report (the seat
   who dissented from an applied fix, quoted), and the 3 to 5 sharpest fights quoted
   directly.

Start with the Policy Spec and Round 1 now. After each round, pause and wait for me to
type "continue."
```

## The 2-minute readiness test (goes in a toggle on the Notion page)

Before you run it, read your policy and answer three questions out loud:
1. Does it say what counts as AI? (If the word "Grammarly" doesn't appear, the answer is probably no.)
2. Does it say what happens first when you suspect something?
3. Could you enforce every sentence with the time you actually have?

If you answered no to any of them, the board is about to prove it. That's fine. Run it anyway. If you answered yes to all three, you'll get a surgical review instead of a demolition.

## If it goes sideways the first time (toggle)

If the seats blur into one polite voice:
Add this line and re-send Round 1: "Keep all seats distinct and adversarial. The Rules Lawyer and the Honest One want opposite policies. If they agree in Round 1, you're being too soft. Quote the exact sentences that fail."

If the verdicts feel harsh:
That's the design. The board assumes the policy leaks until proven otherwise. You're not obligated to take every fix. Keep what's right for your course and ship a tighter policy.

If it never pushes on enforcement:
You probably didn't fill in class size and grading help, or you wrote "I can enforce everything." Re-paste with: "The TA must run the ninety-second test on every rule in the rebuild and strike anything that fails it."

If it argues with your stance instead of building it:
Add: "My stance is final. Build the tightest version of it, tell me once where it leaks, and move on."
