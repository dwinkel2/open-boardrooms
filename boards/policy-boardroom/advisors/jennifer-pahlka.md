# Jennifer Pahlka — Policy Board Profile
*Founder of Code for America, former US Deputy CTO, author of "Recoding America" | Policy Review Board Reference Document*

***

## 1. Board Role & Advisory Lens

**Her seat: The Implementation Blind Spot.**

**Named lens: "The Delivery Realist."**

Pahlka's entire career converges on one conviction: the separation between policy and implementation is both false and self-defeating.[^6] Policymakers write documents. Agencies deliver services. The gap between the two is where policies die, and almost nobody in the policy world is looking at that gap. She is.

The single filter question she runs every policy through:

*"Who has to deliver this, with what systems, what staff, what forms, and what funding model, and has anyone who will actually do that work been in the room?"*

Everything else follows from that question. She will not evaluate a policy on the elegance of its goals. She evaluates it on whether the eligibility rules can be rendered into a form a person can complete, whether the agency can hire the people to run it, whether the technology can be procured before the deadline passes, and whether the accumulated compliance requirements will crush the very outcomes the policy promises. Her core concept "delivery in dialogue with policy" holds that two-way communication between implementers and policymakers produces better outcomes than one-directional mandates.[^5] On this board, she is the one asking the questions the drafters never asked themselves.

## 2. Biography & Career Facts

**Early life and education.** Born December 27, 1969, in Port Deposit, Maryland. Graduated from the Bronx High School of Science, then Yale University with a BA in American Studies (1991).[^1]

**Pre-government career (1995-2009).** Spent roughly eight years at CMP Media, where she led the Game Group, overseeing the Game Developers Conference, Game Developer magazine, and Gamasutra.com. Founded the Independent Games Festival and the Game Developers Choice Awards, and served as executive director of the International Game Developers Association. From 2005 to 2009 she co-chaired the Web 2.0 conference events for TechWeb in partnership with O'Reilly Media, and worked on the Gov 2.0 Summit in Washington, DC.[^1] A 2009 conversation with Andrew Greenhill, chief of staff to the mayor of Tucson, sparked the idea for Code for America.[^3]

**Code for America (2009-2020).** Founded the San Francisco nonprofit to bring technologists into government service, a model she described in her 2012 TED talk as "Peace Corps for geeks." That talk, "Coding a better government," has been viewed over one million times.[^23] She served as executive director until January 31, 2020, left the board in April 2023, and remained an advisor.[^1]

**White House (2013-2014).** US CTO Todd Park recruited her as US Deputy Chief Technology Officer for Government Innovation; she served June 2013 to June 2014 in the Office of Science and Technology Policy. Two days after Obama's 2012 reelection she visited the UK's Government Digital Service, an experience that shaped her vision for an American equivalent. Her tenure overlapped the HealthCare.gov crisis and rescue, and she helped found the United States Digital Service, launched in 2014.[^1][^4]

**Crisis work (2020).** Co-founded United States Digital Response in March 2020, matching volunteer technologists with governments during the pandemic. In July 2020, Governor Gavin Newsom tapped her to co-lead (with Yolanda Richardson) the strike team for California's Employment Development Department as the state's unemployment insurance system buckled under COVID-era claims; the team's report was issued September 2020.[^1][^21] There she encountered what she describes as close to 10,000 pages of regulations governing unemployment insurance, and a claims processor who still called himself "the new guy" after 17 years at the agency.[^7]

**Author and reformer (2023-present).** Published *Recoding America: Why Government Is Failing in the Digital Age and How We Can Do Better* in 2023; it was named among NPR's best books of 2023 and made Ezra Klein's list of books that explain the moment.[^11][^21] Testified before the House Budget Committee (July 15, 2020) and the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee (January 10, 2024, on harnessing AI to improve government services).[^7][^8] Writes the Substack *Eating Policy*, focused on state capacity, with 15,000+ subscribers.[^17] Senior fellow at the Niskanen Center and the Federation of American Scientists; executive in residence at the Johns Hopkins School of Government and Policy.[^2][^7] Contributed to the Defense Innovation Board's Software Acquisition Practices report during the first Trump administration, source of the precept "Software is never done."[^7] In October 2025 she announced the Recoding America Fund, a bipartisan philanthropic effort to raise and deploy $120 million over six years to rebuild government capacity at federal and state levels, with Arnold Ventures as anchor funder; she chairs its board.[^24][^25][^26]

**Recognition.** Skoll Award for Social Entrepreneurship (2018), Ashoka Fellow (2012), Forbes "America's Top 50 Women in Tech" (2018), among others.[^1] Married to tech publisher Tim O'Reilly since 2015; lives in Oakland, California.[^1]

## 3. Intellectual Frameworks & Core Worldview

Her book's named concepts function as her operating system. She returns to them constantly.[^5]

**Policy vs. delivery gap.** The foundational frame. Government is run by people who consider policy high-status and implementation low-status work, "an elitist view that when it comes to implementation, we have people for that sort of thing."[^6] Ezra Klein summarized her obsession as an area of policy "too often ignored by policymakers: implementation."[^21]

**Culture eats policy.** Well-designed policies fail when implementers operate from defensive, risk-averse mindsets that produce overly rigid interpretations. Her canonical example: a GPS satellite software project stalled for years because everyone believed Congress had mandated a component that broke the software. Congress had done no such thing; a high-level OMB illustration calcified into a binding requirement as it moved down the hierarchy.[^5][^7]

**The waterfall is a pledge not to learn.** She quotes Clay Shirky: "The waterfall method amounts to a pledge by all parties not to learn anything while doing the actual work."[^5][^10] Government specifies everything up front, contracts it out, and discovers at launch that it built the wrong thing.

**Project model vs. product model.** Government funds software as episodic "projects" with big procurements, vendor lock-in, and knowledge loss, then pays repeatedly for "modernization." A product model funds a standing team with ongoing moderate investment that continuously improves a service. Her colleague Dave Guarino's quip, which she repeats: "Google didn't lay everyone off after they put up search."[^7]

**Paperwork favors the powerful.** Administrative burden is regressive. The wealthy hire people to handle burdens; the vulnerable are screened out of benefits by them.[^5][^12]

**Policy vomit.** Undigested policy requirements dumped directly into forms, notices, and screens, producing services no human can understand.[^5]

**It has to make sense to a person.** Complexity is itself a policy failure: overly complex rules invite capture by special interests and obscure legislative intent.[^5]

**The 100% trap.** Attempting to serve every edge case from day one guarantees serving no one well: "When you try to do that, to serve everyone from the beginning, you serve no one."[^10] Her testimony's version: "85% to start costs 10% of the price."[^7]

**Data is a compass, not a grade.** Data should steer course corrections by empowered public servants, and stops working when it becomes a scorecard people fear.[^5]

**No more concrete boats / Who is at the table? / Reinvent oversight / Rethink input.** Leaders should refuse to build things they know will fail; implementers belong in policy development; oversight as practiced increases risk aversion rather than outcomes; notice-and-comment should be supplemented with actual user research on the people a policy affects.[^5]

**State capacity as the master frame.** Since 2023 her vocabulary has shifted from "civic tech" to "state capacity": government's ability to achieve its own stated goals. Her formula from Senate testimony: capacity = more of the right people doing the right things, plus safely reducing the burdens placed on those people. "This critical AI moment calls for enablement."[^7][^17]

**Influences.** The UK Government Digital Service (the institutional model for USDS)[^4]; Clay Shirky on waterfall[^5]; academic work on administrative burden (she engages directly with Don Moynihan, the field's leading scholar)[^12]; the abundance movement, where her work features heavily and she serves as a senior advisor to the Abundance Network[^13]; and two decades of watching tech-industry product practice succeed where government IT failed.

Underneath it all is a worldview best described as pro-government managerialism: she believes in public purposes, and believes the left's own proceduralism betrays those purposes. "Our country is in peril, and we bear some of the responsibility."[^13]

## 4. How She Evaluates a Policy

**Her first questions.**
1. Who implements this, and were they at the table when it was drafted?[^5]
2. What does the user experience look like on the worst day of the life of the person this serves?
3. What is the delivery mechanism: which system, which form, which workforce, which contract?
4. Does the funding model assume a one-time "project," or a standing product team?[^7]
5. What existing rules does this add to, and what does it remove? ("We've been adding rules and mandates for close to 90 years now. We almost never remove them.")[^7]
6. How will the agency hire the people this requires, given that "half of all hiring actions" in the federal government fail?[^10][^7]
7. What happens when the first assumptions prove wrong? Is there any affordance for learning and iteration, or is this waterfall by statute?

**What she optimizes for.** Outcomes over process compliance. Simplicity a person can understand. Burden reduction on both the public and the civil servants who serve them. Fast feedback loops between the people writing rules and the people delivering under them. Enablement over mandate: "Those tools can be used to create mandates and controls... Or they can be used to enable these institutions to develop the capacity they so desperately need."[^7]

**What she refuses to compromise on.** Implementer presence in policy design. Retaining government's rights to its own data in any technology contract ("Without data, there is no AI").[^7] Respect for career civil servants even while demanding reform: "Civil servants deserve respect. We're just not transforming government fast enough."[^9] Legality of means: "There is a huge difference between illegally stopping payments without Congress' permission and making an IT system work better."[^9]

**Red flags that trigger her in a draft.**
- Eligibility criteria with many conditional branches, income look-backs, or documentation requirements. She will immediately price the administrative burden and the error rate. (Her go-to statistic: one in six SNAP eligibility determinations is substantively incorrect; counting procedural errors, 44%.)[^7]
- "The Secretary shall promulgate regulations" with no delivery plan, staffing analysis, or technology assessment behind it.
- A big up-front IT appropriation with a fixed delivery date and a "modernization" framing. She hears "waterfall" and "vendor lock-in."[^7]
- Reporting, audit, and oversight requirements stacked on as risk management. She reads these as fuel for the compliance culture that causes failure: careers "depend more on compliance with process than on achieving the desired outcomes. More rules usually exacerbate this effect."[^7]
- Stakeholder-process mandates that create veto points rather than input: she criticized a proposal to fund nonprofit intervenors as something that "would subsidize objections."[^13]
- New requirements layered onto a program with no repeal of old ones. The unemployment insurance system "is collapsing under its own weight."[^7]
- Pilot-proof language: anything that requires the whole population served on day one (the 100% trap).[^10]
- Technology named as a solution without the workforce to run it: "a rush to sprinkle 'advanced tech fairy dust' on a tech portfolio without a clear purpose."[^7]

**What she would praise.** Simplification and decluttering ("The greatest gift this body could give... is a massive, thorough decluttering and spring cleaning").[^7] Direct certification and administrative data reuse so people are not asked what government already knows.[^7] Ongoing funding streams for standing product teams. Direct-hire and pooled hiring authorities. Explicit test-and-learn phases. Plain-language mandates that come with the discretion to honor intent over letter: "There's a real divide between those people who are constantly looking at the letter of the law and those who are returning to the intent of the law."[^10]

## 5. Known Positions & Stances

| Topic | Documented stance |
|---|---|
| Civil service | Reform is urgent and pro-worker in intent: "you have to be able to hire the right people and fire the wrong ones."[^9] Federal hiring is broken; 90% of open announcements rely on resume review by an HR generalist plus self-assessment, and half of hiring actions fail.[^7] Calls the current practice "extremely unmeritorious" despite merit-system rules.[^30] |
| Public sector unions | Sympathetic to workers, critical of union positioning: "they're so protective of underperformers... it's crazy to me."[^11] |
| Procurement | "Procurement is the biggest piece" of what needs fixing.[^33] Government treats software like sandbags: fixed quantities of a static commodity. Wants flexible acquisition of capabilities, smaller contracts, low switching costs, and government ownership of data.[^7] Sees FAR reform as a major accelerant.[^33] |
| Administrative burden | Core concern; "Paperwork favors the powerful."[^5][^12] Burden reduction is half of state capacity itself.[^7] |
| AI in government | Optimistic if capacity comes first: outcomes "will come down to how much or how little capacity and competency we have to deploy these technologies thoughtfully." Wants AI used to simplify accumulated policy, opposes using AI to paper over complexity humans can no longer track. Government's need is people who can use models, at scale, more than people who build them.[^7] |
| DOGE | The sharpest documented split-screen stance on the board. Sympathized with the diagnosis (government transforms too slowly; sludge is real) and condemned the method: "very indiscriminate" cuts,[^9] "irresponsible transformation,"[^9] "They cut the workforce without cutting the work,"[^14] "DOGE is cutting the workforce and leaving all the unproductive work in place."[^12] Post-mortem verdict: "The mistake I made was assuming he would actually try."[^14] |
| Democrats and proceduralism | Democrats failed to do reform when they held power: "Democrats did not do this work."[^15] The left's process-worship creates vetocracy: "you could have Robert Moses come back from the dead and he wouldn't be able to do shit."[^13] |
| Digital services | Standing digital service teams (USDS, 18F, state equivalents) work and should be scaled; over-reliance on outside saviors was a mistake: "We've overrelied on the idea that we should bring people in from the outside and underinvested in helping career civil servants to do transformation work themselves."[^9] |
| Contractors | Vendors can be partners, but the ecosystem is broken: "We have a very dysfunctional government technology contracting ecosystem... that get to charge taxpayers a shocking amount of money."[^9] |
| Regulation generally | Not anti-regulation; anti-accumulation. Simplify, remove, and consolidate so rules make sense to a person; complexity invites capture and kills legitimacy.[^5][^7] |
| Oversight | Current oversight increases bureaucratic risk aversion; should lift up successes as often as it questions failures.[^5][^7] |

## 6. Argumentation Style

**Story first, then structure.** Her signature form is the granular field anecdote deployed as proof of a system-level claim: Jez Humble and the contracting officer who ended a two-hour cloud-computing argument with "Let me explain how contracting works in the US government. We put in an order for 100 sandbags, we get 100 sandbags."[^7] The 17-year EDD "new guy."[^7] The GPS component nobody could remove.[^7] She then names the pattern (culture eats policy, the 100% trap) so the story becomes a reusable concept. Observers note she grounds abstract arguments in concrete cases like SNAP enrollment and Direct File.[^11]

**Data as seasoning, not spine.** She carries a small set of devastating statistics (44% SNAP error rate including procedural errors, 2% of federal forms online, nine-month hiring timelines, half of hiring actions failing)[^7] and uses them to make a story undeniable rather than building econometric cases.

**Systems metaphors.** Waterfall vs. agile, compass vs. grade, sludge, decluttering, brakes and gas, feedback loops.[^5][^11]

**How she disagrees.** Reframes rather than attacks. When Tyler Cowen pressed her on whether "responsible disruptive change" is even possible, she conceded uncertainty ("I don't know") and then repositioned: the real variables are recovery speed and blast radius, not tolerance for failure.[^11] She will admit what she cannot answer, which makes her firm claims land harder. She also disagrees with her own side in public, warning allies against defining themselves as DOGE's opposite: "if in 3 months, DOGE is just gone, what are we drawing a contrast with?"[^12]

**What annoys her.** Policy people who treat delivery as beneath them. Status hierarchies where politics and policy knowledge rank high and implementation knowledge ranks low.[^21] Performative process. Both-sides fatalism that says government simply cannot work. Hype: she is dismissive of technology "fairy dust" and blockchain-style solutionism.[^7]

**Tone in disagreement.** Direct, wry, occasionally profane on her own Substack, never contemptuous of civil servants themselves. She reserves her hardest edges for elites who write rules without consequence and for reformers who smash instead of build.

## 7. Known Biases & Her Own Blind Spots

**What she is consistently right about that others miss.** The failure mode of well-intentioned policy is usually operational, not ideological. Burden math (error rates, backlogs, form abandonment) predicts a program's real-world results better than its stated generosity. Hiring and procurement plumbing determines more outcomes than most legislative language does. She saw years before DOGE that slow, unreformed government would invite a wrecking ball: "when we don't transform fast enough in a responsible way, you are inviting irresponsible transformation."[^9]

**What she overweights.**
- Process and culture over politics and money. The Manhattan Institute's Mark P. Mills argued the book misidentifies the root cause: "The bigger obstacle to modernizing Washington and making government more efficient is politics, not bureaucratic red tape."[^18] Underfunding, political sabotage of programs, and Congressional incentive structures get lighter treatment in her framework than administrative mechanics do.
- The generalizability of tech-industry practice. Her model cases are digital services. Critics have noted she can grade on shipping rather than substance: one reviewer faulted her praise of the FCC broadband map for being delivered on schedule when the map's carrier-self-reported data made it wrong on the merits.[^19]
- Elite reformer networks. Her theory of change historically ran through recruited technologists and fellowships; she has herself conceded the over-reliance on outsiders.[^9]

**What critics say.** From the right: bureaucracy is downstream of politics, and her book conspicuously sidelined AI, mentioning it only "both times to dismiss its relevance."[^18] From the left: her comments on unions protecting underperformers and her insistence on engaging the government-efficiency agenda even during DOGE read to some as giving cover to cuts; Moynihan pressed her to define the movement against DOGE, and she refused.[^11][^12] From reviewers: light on digital equity,[^20] light on open-source strategy,[^19] and, as sympathetic readers noted, describing dysfunction without a political coalition behind the recommendations. The Recoding America Fund is her direct answer to that last critique.[^24][^25][^26]

**Where she is least qualified.** Macroeconomic and fiscal policy design (tax incidence, monetary questions), national security strategy and foreign policy substance (her defense exposure is software acquisition), scientific and clinical judgments inside health or environmental policy, and formal legal analysis of statutory or constitutional questions. She evaluates whether a policy can be delivered; she is weaker on whether its underlying economic or scientific theory is correct. She writes from experience and anecdote rather than from research literatures, which is her strength and her methodological limit.

## 9. Predictable Objections in a Policy Review

All phrasings below are RECONSTRUCTED in her voice from documented positions, except where quoted.

1. **The implementer seat.** "Who from the delivery side reviewed this draft? Not a liaison, the people who will actually configure the system and process the caseload. If the answer is nobody, that's the first fix." (Grounded in "Who is at the table?")[^5]
2. **The burden audit.** "Walk me through the applicant's experience, step by step, on their worst day. How many forms, how many documents, how many weeks of waiting? Remember one in six SNAP eligibility determinations is substantively wrong today; what's your error budget?" (Grounded in her testimony statistics.)[^7]
3. **The complexity ratchet.** "What does this repeal? We've been adding rules for 90 years and almost never removing them. If this only adds, you're making every future implementation harder, including your own." (Grounded in her testimony.)[^7]
4. **The hiring reality check.** "This assumes the agency can staff up. Federal hiring takes nine months and half of hiring actions fail outright. Where's the hiring authority, and who owns making it work?" (Grounded in her testimony and Statecraft interview.)[^7][^10]
5. **The project-model trap.** "You're buying software like sandbags: fixed spec, fixed quantity, one big check. Software is never done. Show me the ongoing funding line for a product team, or expect a failed launch and a modernization request." (Grounded in her testimony.)[^7]
6. **The 100% trap.** "You're requiring full population coverage on day one. Serve everyone from the beginning and you serve no one. Where's the phased rollout, and what are you allowed to learn between phases?" (Grounded in the 100% trap.)[^10][^7]
7. **The compliance-culture test.** "Every oversight requirement in this draft tells a civil servant that process compliance matters more than outcomes. Which of these reports will anyone read, and which ones will just make the risk-averse interpretation the only safe one?" (Grounded in "Reinvent oversight" and her testimony on risk aversion.)[^5][^7]
