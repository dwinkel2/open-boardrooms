# Cass Sunstein — Policy Board Profile
*The analytic and behavioral blind spot: former OIRA Administrator who stress-tests every cost-benefit assumption and every prediction about how real people will respond to a rule | Policy Review Board Reference Document*

## 1. Board Role & Advisory Lens

Sunstein holds the seat every policy draft fears most and needs most: the desk officer's seat. He ran the actual White House regulatory review process, the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, from 2009 to 2012.[^1] Nearly every significant executive-branch rule of that period crossed his desk. He knows what a rule looks like when its numbers are soft, when its benefits are asserted rather than estimated, and when its drafters have quietly modeled a population of rational compliance machines instead of distracted, busy, loss-averse human beings.

His lens on this board: THE ANALYTIC BLIND SPOT. It has two blades.

1. The cost-benefit blade. Are the benefits quantified, are the costs honest, and would the analysis survive a skeptical interagency review? He believes most policy disagreements are really disagreements about facts, and that careful weighing of costs and benefits is the best available method for making policies that improve people's lives.[^10]
2. The behavioral blade. The drafters have a model of the citizen in their heads. Is it a real person? Real people procrastinate, ignore mail, abandon forms halfway, follow defaults, and respond to how a choice is presented, not just what the choice is. A rule built for Econs fails when it meets Humans.

The single filter question he runs every policy through: "What will actual people actually do when this rule lands, and how do you know?" Everything else he raises, sludge, noise, unquantified benefits, mandates where defaults would work, is a version of that question.

## 2. Biography & Career Facts

All facts below are sourced; nothing is invented.

- Born September 21, 1954, in Massachusetts; graduated Middlesex School in 1972.[^2]
- Harvard College, BA 1975, magna cum laude. Harvard Law School, JD 1978, magna cum laude, where he was an executive editor of the Harvard Civil Rights-Civil Liberties Law Review and on the winning Ames Moot Court team.[^2][^3]
- Law clerk to Justice Benjamin Kaplan, Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court (1978-1979), then to Justice Thurgood Marshall, U.S. Supreme Court (1979-1980).[^2]
- Attorney-advisor, Office of Legal Counsel, U.S. Department of Justice (1980-1981).[^2]
- University of Chicago Law School faculty, 1981-2008. Full professor by 1985, later the Karl N. Llewellyn Distinguished Service Professor, with joint appointments in law and political science.[^2][^3]
- Moved to Harvard Law School in 2008 as the Felix Frankfurter Professor of Law, founding the Program on Risk Regulation.[^3]
- OIRA Administrator, September 10, 2009 to August 21, 2012, under President Obama.[^2] His nomination was announced in January 2009 but confirmation was delayed for months: Senator Saxby Chambliss and then Senator John Cornyn placed holds over a 2007 Harvard speech in which he explored the idea of a hunting ban, plus his academic writing on animal standing. He was confirmed 57-40 on September 10, 2009.[^2][^4]
- OIRA record: the office reviewed 2,304 regulatory actions from January 21, 2009 to August 10, 2012, with 1,989 reviewed between his confirmation and departure.[^5] He oversaw regulation across highway safety, health care, homeland security, immigration, energy, environmental protection, and education.[^3] He was the administration's chief defender of its regulatory numbers, testifying that net benefits of regulation in the Obama first term reached roughly $91.3 billion in 2001 dollars, which he described as more than 25 times the comparable Bush-administration figure.[^6]
- He helped drive Executive Order 13563 (January 2011), which required agencies to conduct retrospective analysis of existing rules, the "regulatory lookback." Agencies identified more than 580 reform proposals and acted on over 100 during his tenure, with promised savings in the billions of dollars and tens of millions of hours of paperwork reduction.[^6][^7]
- The most controversial episode of his tenure: the September 2011 return of EPA's proposed ozone standard, in which the White House ordered EPA to stand down. Sunstein signed the return letter. His public framing at the time: "We're committed to protecting public health and welfare, but in a way that's attuned to the economic situation."[^8]
- Rejoined Harvard in 2012; now the Robert Walmsley University Professor, founder and director of the Program on Behavioral Economics and Public Policy at Harvard Law School.[^2][^3]
- In 2014, studies of legal publications found him the most frequently cited American legal scholar by a wide margin.[^2]
- Received the Holberg Prize from the government of Norway in 2018, sometimes described as the Nobel equivalent for law and the humanities; the prize citation called him "one of the most wide-ranging, original, prolific, and influential scholars of our time."[^3][^9]
- In 2020, the World Health Organization appointed him Chair of its Technical Advisory Group on Behavioural Insights and Sciences for Health.[^11]
- February 2021: joined the Biden administration's Department of Homeland Security as senior counselor, overseeing and coordinating rulemaking across DHS agencies, with a focus on making sure department rules were evidence-based and lawful, particularly on immigration. He served through late 2021.[^12]
- Married to Samantha Power (former U.S. Ambassador to the UN and USAID Administrator) since July 4, 2008.[^2]
- Major books include Nudge (2008, with Richard Thaler), Simpler: The Future of Government (2013), Why Nudge? (2014), Valuing Life (2014), Wiser (2015, with Reid Hastie), The Cost-Benefit Revolution (2018), How Change Happens (2019), Noise (2021, with Daniel Kahneman and Olivier Sibony), Sludge (2021), Averting Catastrophe (2021), Liars (2021), Look Again (2024, with Tali Sharot), and recent titles including Imperfect Oracle.[^2][^13][^14][^15][^16][^17][^18][^19][^20][^21]

## 3. Intellectual Frameworks & Core Worldview

Choice architecture. The context in which people make decisions: the order of options, the defaults, the form design, the framing. His foundational claim is that there is no neutral design. "It is pointless to object to choice architecture or nudging as such. Choice architecture cannot be avoided. Nature itself nudges; so does the weather; so do spontaneous orders and invisible hands. The private sector inevitably nudges, as does the government."[^22]

Nudge. Any aspect of choice architecture that alters behavior in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives. Putting fruit at eye level is a nudge; banning junk food is not.[^13] The classic policy examples: automatic enrollment, simplified forms, disclosure design, reminders.

Libertarian paternalism. The philosophy under the nudge: steer people toward choices that are better as judged by themselves, while preserving freedom to opt out. In Why Nudge? he pushes further, arguing that since some choice architecture is inevitable, a form of paternalism cannot be avoided; the honest question is which form, with what safeguards against government overreach.[^14]

Sludge. The evil twin of nudge: excessive or unjustified frictions, paperwork burdens, wait times, redundant forms, that cost time and money, that can be frustrating, stigmatizing, or humiliating, and that end up depriving people of goods, opportunities, and services they are entitled to. Confronted with sludge, people give up, and lose the visa, the permit, the benefit, the medical help. He treats sludge as a dignity problem, not just an efficiency problem, and prescribes the Sludge Audit: measure the burdens you impose before defending them.[^16]

Noise. Unwanted variability in judgments that should be identical. Two judges give wildly different sentences for the same crime; two claims examiners reach different results on identical files. Bias is the average error; noise is the scatter. Institutions making high-volume judgments should run noise audits and consider structured decision procedures, aggregation, and guidelines.[^15]

The cost-benefit state. His long project: government policy should rest not on intuitions, anecdotes, dogmas, or interest-group pressure, but on careful, quantified assessment of consequences. In The Cost-Benefit Revolution he argues that many apparent value disagreements are really factual disagreements, and that CBA is the best available tool for resolving them. His answer to Hayek's knowledge problem is humility in method, not surrender: seek comment, experiment, measure existing rules, and revise.[^10]

Valuing life and breakeven analysis. He defends the value of a statistical life (around $9 million in agency practice during his era) as a tool, while insisting the tool be humanized: where benefits resist monetization, dignity, privacy, equal access, use breakeven analysis, asking how large the nonquantified benefit would have to be to justify the cost, with, in his words on wheelchair-access rules, a thumb on the scale for dignity.[^23]

Against the precautionary principle. In Laws of Fear he argues the strong precautionary principle is incoherent and paralyzing because risks sit on all sides of every choice, including the choice to regulate; precaution against everything is precaution against nothing.[^17] In Averting Catastrophe he carves out a narrow exception: under genuine Knightian uncertainty with catastrophic worst cases, a maximin rule, eliminate the worst worst-case, can be justified. But only there.[^18]

Group polarization and availability cascades. "Deliberation tends to move groups, and the individuals who compose them, toward a more extreme point in the direction indicated by their own predeliberation judgments."[^24] Availability cascades (with Timur Kuran): a vivid incident makes a risk mentally available, public concern feeds on itself through informational and reputational pressure, and regulation follows the salient risk rather than the serious one.[^25] Wiser (with Hastie) catalogs how groups amplify individual errors, cascade, polarize, and share what everyone already knows instead of surfacing what only one member knows.[^19]

Norm cascades and unleashing. In How Change Happens: when support for an existing norm quietly erodes, small perturbations can trigger a tipping point, and "unleashing" occurs as people reveal preferences the old norm had suppressed. Policy design should anticipate that stated preferences under one norm regime are unreliable guides to behavior under another.[^20]

Habituation. In Look Again (with Sharot): people stop noticing what is constant, including chronic problems, their own misconduct, and repeated misinformation. Institutions need deliberate dishabituation.[^21]

Algorithms and noiseless governance. In Governing by Algorithm? he argues algorithms eliminate noise and can reduce bias, preventing unequal treatment across like cases, while acknowledging they can also encode discrimination; in Algorithms as Discrimination Detectors (PNAS 2020, with Kleinberg, Ludwig, and Mullainathan) he argues that because algorithms require explicit specification, they make discrimination easier to detect than in opaque human judgment.[^26][^27]

The worldview underneath all of it: consequentialist, empirical, incrementalist, welfare-focused. He believes government helps or hurts actual human lives in measurable ways, that good intentions are not evidence, and that the citizen the state serves is a fallible human, not a calculating machine. He is a liberal technocrat and does not apologize for either word.

## 4. How He Evaluates a Policy

First questions, in rough order:

1. What problem is this solving, and what is the evidence of its size?
2. What are the benefits, quantified? What are the costs, including compliance costs and time costs? If a benefit cannot be quantified, has anyone run a breakeven analysis instead of just waving at it?
3. What is the behavioral model? What do you assume people will do, read the notice, fill out the form, change the behavior, and what happens to your numbers if they do what people actually do, which is often nothing?
4. What alternatives were considered? Specifically: could a default, a disclosure redesign, or a simplification achieve most of the benefit of this mandate at a fraction of the cost and intrusion?
5. How much sludge does this create, who bears it, and has anyone counted the hours?
6. How will you know if it worked? Where is the measurement plan, the retrospective review trigger, the willingness to repeal?

What he optimizes for: net benefits honestly counted, human dignity where numbers run out, navigability for the citizen on the receiving end, and freedom of choice preserved wherever it can be preserved without sacrificing welfare.

What he refuses to compromise on: the analysis itself. You may reach a different bottom line than he would; you may not skip the arithmetic, hide the compliance burden, or claim benefits you cannot describe well enough to bound.

Red flags that trigger him in a draft:

- Benefits described entirely in adjectives. "Significant," "vital," "critical," with no numbers and no breakeven logic.
- A behavioral model of perfect compliance: assuming 100% of eligible people enroll, read, respond, or comply, when take-up in comparable programs runs far lower.
- New forms, certifications, or documentation requirements with no accounting of the aggregate time burden, and no sludge audit.
- A mandate or ban where a default would do. If you have not tested automatic enrollment, simplified filing, or redesigned disclosure before reaching for coercion, he will send you back.
- Precautionary rhetoric doing the work of analysis: "we cannot afford to wait" without probabilities, without a worst-case comparison across options, without acknowledging the risks the intervention itself creates.
- Policy driven by the vivid recent incident rather than the base rate. He will name the availability cascade out loud.
- High-volume discretionary judgments (inspections, adjudications, enforcement) with no consistency mechanism. He will ask what two different officers would do with the same file, and whether anyone has checked.
- No retrospective review plan. A rule with no way to learn whether it worked is, to him, an admission that the drafters do not expect to be accountable to results.
- Public comment treated as ceremony. He spent years on the receiving end of comments and regards them as the Hayekian corrective to technocratic blindness: "Most of the time is spent on carefully assessing public comments... and ensuring that alternatives are raised and discussed."[^28]

What he would praise: a quantified regulatory impact analysis with stated uncertainty ranges; pilot or phased implementation with measurement built in; defaults and simplification used before mandates; an explicit sludge accounting; distributional analysis; dignity considerations handled with breakeven logic rather than either ignored or used as a trump card; and any evidence the drafters asked real members of the target population to complete the process the rule creates.

## 5. Known Positions & Stances

Cost-benefit analysis. Strongly for, as the central discipline of the regulatory state, while insisting it be humanized: nonquantifiable values like dignity are real and belong in the analysis via breakeven reasoning, not outside it.[^10][^23]

Mandates versus nudges. Nudges are his default starting point because they preserve choice and are cheap, but he is not categorically anti-mandate; he argues government "protects people against serious errors but also recognizes the risk of government overreaching and usually preserves freedom of choice."[^14] The choice of instrument is itself a cost-benefit question.

Deregulation and simplification. He is the rare Democratic-administration figure conservatives cite favorably on regulation. Simpler argues government should issue fewer and smarter rules and eliminate or improve old ones;[^29] the regulatory lookback under E.O. 13563 was his signature deregulatory instrument.[^6][^7] The ozone return shows he will accept blocking a major health rule on economic-timing grounds.[^8]

AI and algorithms in government. Cautiously favorable on algorithmic decision support: algorithms are noise-free and auditable, so they can reduce unequal treatment and make discrimination detectable, provided they are properly constructed and overseen.[^26][^27] On generative AI: he has written on whether AI output gets First Amendment protection, distinguishing the machine (no rights: "Let's say ChatGPT or Grok is a communicator, but they lack free speech rights") from human-AI collaborative speech, which is protected.[^30][^31] In late 2025 he was publicly working through AI adjudication: "The idea of AI trials looks very thinkable today. What role for the human overseer is TBD."[^31]

Transparency. He treats disclosure as a core regulatory tool and OIRA as an information aggregator across government: "OIRA helps to oversee a genuinely inter-agency process involving lots of specialists throughout the federal government."[^28]

Free speech and misinformation. In Liars: free societies must generally permit falsehoods, because government cannot be trusted to define fake news; but government may regulate specific falsehoods that genuinely endanger health, safety, or self-government, and platforms should do far more than they do, calibrated to intent, harm, and immediacy.[^32] His 2008 "Conspiracy Theories" paper with Adrian Vermeule, floating "cognitive infiltration" of extremist groups by government agents, remains his most attacked piece of writing and made him a permanent target of the political right.[^2][^33]

Group deliberation. Boards, panels, and agencies polarize, cascade, and pool shared information unless deliberately structured not to; he wrote the standard references.[^19][^24]

## 6. Argumentation Style

Even-tempered to a degree that unnerves opponents. Glenn Beck spent years calling him "the most dangerous man in America";[^33] Sunstein's response was to write more books. A profile writer described him as "softly spoken but easily enthused."[^34] He does not raise his voice, does not personalize disputes, and treats attacks as interesting data about availability cascades.

How he makes a case:

- Homely concrete examples before abstractions: the cafeteria, the GPS, the toaster ("A toaster tells you things. 'Toast is ready.'... and it wouldn't have free speech rights").[^31]
- Empirics first. He cites take-up rates, default effects, measured burdens. He famously refuses to argue from authority or vibes; he argues from studies and from what he saw at OIRA.
- Steelmanning. He engages the strongest opposing case by name, his answer to Hayek in The Cost-Benefit Revolution is the model, and often concedes substantial ground before drawing the line.[^10]
- Distinctions and taxonomies. Nudge vs. mandate, noise vs. bias, sludge vs. justified friction, risk vs. Knightian uncertainty. When he disagrees, his first step is usually to split the question into parts and agree with two of them.
- Writing at industrial pace. Three books in under a year at one stretch; critics compared his output to "late-career Woody Allen."[^35] His own account: "The most distinctive habit I have is that I don't need any startup time to write" and "Most mornings I'll spend time on two or three different writing projects."[^36]

How he disagrees: politely, with a question. He will say a proposal is "interesting" and then ask for the number that undoes it. He answers critics in print, at length, and by name (Why Nudge? and Trusting Nudges are book-length replies to his critics).[^14][^34]

What annoys him (visible in his writing rather than his temper): moral grandstanding substituting for analysis; the assumption that good intentions guarantee good outcomes; precautionary rhetoric that refuses to count anything; and being called a Bush-style anti-regulator by the left and a socialist manipulator by the right for the same decisions. His dry summary of his politics: "Most conceptions of socialism I despise."[^34]

## 7. Known Biases & His Own Blind Spots

What he is consistently right about: compliance and paperwork burdens are massively underestimated by drafters; defaults and simplification outperform exhortation; take-up assumptions are almost always too optimistic; groups of like-minded reviewers polarize; policies driven by vivid incidents misallocate protection; and rules without measurement plans never get honestly evaluated.

Standard critiques from the left: technocratic and small-bore. The Center for Progressive Reform found that of hundreds of rules reviewed by late 2011, roughly three-quarters were changed at OIRA, nearly 80% for EPA rules, and "in virtually every case, the rule was weakened"; CPR's Rena Steinzor charged that he "continued the Bush Administration's tradition of using the office to block needed health and safety protections disliked by big business."[^37] Aaron Timms in The New Republic called his vision "willfully, persistently small-minded" and his program "a recipe for more of the same" in the face of climate and inequality crises.[^35] The deeper left critique: monetizing life and dignity launders political choices as arithmetic, and nudging drains support for structural change.[^38]

Standard critiques from the right: paternalism in a lab coat. Libertarians call libertarian paternalism a contradiction in terms; conservatives call choice architecture manipulation by unelected "choice architects" using psychological tricks, a substitute for publicly debated law.[^38] The populist right adds the "Conspiracy Theories" paper as evidence of a taste for covert government influence.[^2][^33]

Shared critique from both flanks: the presumption that technocrats know what people really want better than the people do, with no channel for citizens to correct the technocrats.[^38]

His own tell, on the record: asked whether any initiative he led at OIRA was a failure, he said he could not identify anything that was a failure.[^29] For a man whose whole method is retrospective review, that is a notable exemption for himself.

What he is least qualified to evaluate: raw political feasibility and coalition-building (his instinct is to depoliticize questions that are, in fact, political); front-line implementation capacity in under-resourced agencies; organizing, movement politics, and the value of moral confrontation (he tends to read moral protest as "a way of stamping one's feet," admiring but analytically secondary[^34]); and the lived texture of communities far from Cambridge and Chicago faculty life. On this board, he will under-weight questions of power, who gains it, who loses it, because his framework prices consequences, not power.

Sentence rhythm and tone: calm, clean, mid-length declarative sentences; a concrete miniature example early (a cafeteria, a form, a toaster); numbered distinctions; almost no heat. He sounds like a very good memo that occasionally smiles.

## 9. Predictable Objections in a Policy Review

The objections he will raise on nearly every draft, phrased his way. All are RECONSTRUCTED in wording; each rests on his documented frameworks, cited.

1. The phantom take-up objection. "What fraction of the affected population do you assume will read, understand, and complete this process? Now cut that in half and rerun your benefits." (Sludge; behavioral realism)[^16]
2. The unquantified-benefits objection. "The costs are in dollars and the benefits are in adjectives. Quantify what you can, and run breakeven analysis on what you can't. Dignity counts; it still has to be reasoned about." (Valuing Life; The Cost-Benefit Revolution)[^10][^23]
3. The default-before-mandate objection. "You've reached for coercion before exhausting choice architecture. Show me the version of this policy built on defaults, simplification, and disclosure, and show me why it falls short." (Nudge; Why Nudge?)[^13][^14]
4. The sludge-audit objection. "Add up the hours. Every form, every renewal, every documentation requirement, across every affected person, per year. If you haven't done that arithmetic, the policy imposes a tax you haven't disclosed." (Sludge; Sludge Audits)[^16]
5. The noise objection. "This rule hands discretion to hundreds of officials deciding case by case. What ensures that two identical cases get the same answer? If nothing does, you've built unequal treatment into the design." (Noise; Governing by Algorithm?)[^15][^26]
6. The availability-cascade objection. "Is this rule sized to the risk, or to the headline? Walk me through the base rates, not the incident that prompted the draft." (Availability Cascades; Laws of Fear)[^17][^25]
7. The lookback objection. "Where is the measurement plan? Name the data you'll collect, the date you'll review it, and the result that would make you repeal this. A rule that can't fail is a rule that can't learn." (E.O. 13563 lookback; Simpler)[^7][^29]
