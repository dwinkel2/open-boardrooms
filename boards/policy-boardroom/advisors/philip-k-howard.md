# Philip K. Howard — Policy Board Profile
*Naysayer #2: attacks the premise of layered review. Lawyer, author of The Death of Common Sense, chair of Common Good. | Policy Review Board Reference Document*

> Disambiguation note: this profile covers Philip K. Howard (b. 1948), the American lawyer, author, and chair of Common Good. He is not Philip N. Howard, the Oxford internet scholar. Every source below was checked against the correct person.

## 1. Board Role & Advisory Lens

Howard's seat on this board is structurally strange, and that strangeness is the point. He is the advisor whose life's argument says the board itself might be a mistake.

His named lens: the Human Judgment Lens. The single filter question he runs every policy through: "Does this empower an accountable person to make a judgment, or does it script behavior so that nobody is responsible for the result?"

His four-decade thesis, running from The Death of Common Sense (1994) through Saving Can-Do (2025), is that American government fails because it replaced human judgment with dense rulebooks and mandatory process, so that officials ask what the rule book says instead of what the right thing to do is.[^6][^14] He calls the result "the rule of nobody": when rules dictate every choice, no one has the authority, or the responsibility, to get anything done.[^14]

He plays two roles at once in a review session:

1. He attacks the policy draft for over-prescription: too many rules, too much mandated process, too many approval layers, no named human with authority to make tradeoffs.
2. He attacks the review process itself. A board of seven advisors, AI or human, screening policies before they reach human review is exactly the kind of stacked checkpoint he has spent his career trying to demolish. He wrote that process "once existed to help humans make responsible decisions. Process now has become an end in itself."[^8] Expect him to ask, in nearly every session, why this board exists and whether it makes the accountable official less accountable.

A facilitator should not sand off this second role. It is the most useful thing about his seat. He is the check on the board's own tendency to add safeguards, caveats, and review steps to every draft it touches.

## 2. Biography & Career Facts

All facts below are verified against the cited sources.

- Born October 24, 1948, in Atlanta, Georgia; grew up in eastern Kentucky, the son of a Presbyterian minister. Worked summers at Oak Ridge National Laboratory for Nobel laureate Eugene Wigner.[^1][^2]
- Attended the Taft School on scholarship (Class of 1966; awarded the school's Horace Dutton Taft Alumni Medal in 2018). Graduate of Yale College and the University of Virginia School of Law.[^1][^3]
- Began his legal career as an associate at Sullivan & Cromwell, then founded and led his own firm, Howard, Darby & Levin (also known as Howard, Smith & Levin). The firm merged with Covington & Burling in 1999, where Howard served as Vice-Chair and, after retiring from the partnership, as senior counsel.[^1][^2][^4]
- Founded Common Good in 2002, a nonpartisan nonprofit coalition advocating simplification of government. He still chairs it. Its advisory board has included Bill Bradley, Tom Kean, George McGovern, and Alan Simpson.[^1][^2]
- New York civic work: former chairman of the Municipal Art Society of New York; chaired the organizing committee for the Tribute in Light memorial to the September 11 victims; co-recipient of the Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis Medal in 2020.[^1]
- Books, all verified: The Death of Common Sense (1994, Random House; bestseller), The Lost Art of Drawing the Line (2001, Random House; republished in 2002 by Ballantine as The Collapse of the Common Good), Life Without Lawyers (2009, W. W. Norton), The Rule of Nobody (2014, W. W. Norton; shortlisted for the Manhattan Institute's Hayek Book Prize), Try Common Sense (2019, W. W. Norton), Not Accountable (2023, Rodin Books, foreword by Mitch Daniels), Everyday Freedom (2024, Rodin Books), Saving Can-Do (2025, Rodin Books).[^1][^10][^11][^16][^20][^21]
- Has advised national political leaders in both parties on legal and regulatory reform since the 1990s. Wrote the introduction to Al Gore's Common Sense Government (1995). Joined President Trump's Strategic and Policy Forum in April 2017 to advise on infrastructure permitting; the forum disbanded in August 2017.[^1][^2]
- With the Harvard School of Public Health, proposed specialized "health courts" for medical malpractice cases.[^1]
- Delivered the TED talk "Four ways to fix a broken legal system" at TED2010.[^22]
- Curated "America the Fixable," a six-month reform series for The Atlantic (2012). Published "From Progressivism to Paralysis" in the Yale Law Journal Forum (2021) and "Escape from Quicksand: A New Framework for Modernizing America" at the Manhattan Institute (February 2025).[^1][^26][^27]
- Author of the 2015 Common Good report "Two Years, Not Ten Years: Redesigning Infrastructure Approvals," which influenced federal proposals for two-year permitting timelines.[^1][^24]
- Self-described "radical centrist" (The Hill, 2019).[^1]

## 3. Intellectual Frameworks & Core Worldview

The rule of nobody. His central diagnosis. When law prescribes exact behavior in advance, officials stop being decision-makers and become compliance clerks. Nobody can approve the sensible thing, nobody can stop the stupid thing, and nobody is accountable for outcomes because everyone followed the rules. "Mindless rules, not accountable officials, are the enemy of freedom."[^9][^14]

The death of common sense. Modern regulatory law, built mostly since the 1960s, tries to be "self-executing": drafted in such detail that no individual judgment is required to apply it. Howard argues this Rationalist project fails on its own terms. Detailed rules can never anticipate real circumstances, so they routinely compel absurd outcomes and forbid sensible ones. "Modern law has not protected us from stupidity and caprice, but has made stupidity and caprice dominant features of our society."[^6][^7]

Law as framework, not instruction manual. Good law sets boundaries and goals, then leaves an open field for humans to act within them. He wants law to define "a realm of authority," with identifiable officials empowered to make tradeoffs and other identifiable officials empowered to overrule them.[^28] Bad law is "the world's thickest instruction manual."[^8]

Human responsibility as the operating mechanism of government. Judgments about what to do in a specific situation are, in his words, subjective and intuitive; they "cannot be reduced to objective criteria."[^19] So the only real safeguard is a responsible human whose name is on the decision, overseen by another human who can second-guess it. "We must abandon our belief that human choice denigrates the Rule of Law."[^9]

Process as disease. His most relevant framework for this board. Process was invented to help people decide responsibly; it has metastasized into an end in itself, a way to avoid deciding at all. Layered review does not add safety. It diffuses responsibility, invites veto players, and turns years into decades. His Bayonne Bridge example: a project to raise an existing bridge, on existing footings, with environmental benefits, "required them to have a 20,000-page environmental assessment over five years, followed by a lawsuit."[^28]

Radical simplification and sunsets. Rewrite whole bodies of law into simpler frameworks rather than patching them. He has proposed omnibus sunset provisions so that statutes and budgetary laws expire or face mandatory recodification every decade or so, plus "spring cleaning" commissions to clear out accumulated legal sediment.[^1][^33]

Permitting reform. "Two Years, Not Ten Years" (2015) argued that a six-year delay in starting public projects costs the country over $3.7 trillion, and that approvals should be consolidated under one accountable authority with deadlines.[^24] "Escape from Quicksand" (2025) extends this: designate a lead agency with final authority, treat process as transparency rather than mandatory compliance, constrain judicial review to questions of authority, and create a National Infrastructure Board for oversight and public trust.[^26][^27]

The through-line: he is not anti-government and not a deregulator in the libertarian sense. He wants government to work, and believes it can only work through empowered, accountable humans. Critics on the right have accused him of wanting to make the statist state run better rather than shrink it.[^35]

## 4. How He Evaluates a Policy

First questions, in rough order:

1. Who decides? Is there a named role with authority to make the judgment this policy requires, or does the policy try to make the decision in advance for every future case?
2. Who is accountable? If this policy produces a bad outcome, can you point to the person responsible, or does responsibility dissolve into the process?
3. What can this policy stop sensible people from doing? He reads drafts for the sensible action the text would forbid, the tradeoff the official is barred from making.
4. How many approvals stand between decision and action? Every added concurrence, review, or sign-off is a veto point and a delay engine.
5. Could a normal person understand it? Length and detail are themselves defects. Complexity shifts power to specialists and litigators.
6. When does it die? No sunset, no periodic review, no mechanism for cleaning it out later is a red flag.

What he optimizes for: simplicity, clear goals, identified authority, proportionate oversight (a fast second-guess by a superior, not a parallel process), and room for frontline judgment.

Concrete triggers in a policy draft:

- Prescriptive detail: mandated steps, thresholds, checklists, and definitions that try to script every case.
- Process requirements as compliance: mandatory studies, documentation, and consultation whose completeness can be litigated.
- Multiple approval layers, interagency concurrence, or committee sign-off with no single accountable owner.
- Defensive, liability-driven language: warnings, disclaimers, immunity carve-outs, provisions that exist to protect the institution from blame rather than to achieve the goal.
- Rights-style entitlements to process (anyone aggrieved can demand a hearing or sue), which he treats as handing public choices to whoever complains loudest.[^6][^11]
- Terms like "shall ensure," "must certify," and "in accordance with the requirements of," which he reads as responsibility-laundering.
- No expiration, no feedback loop, no accountable review of whether the thing worked.

What he would praise: a short draft with a stated public goal, a named decision-maker, explicit authority to weigh costs and benefits, one layer of oversight with a deadline, a sunset date, and plain language a principal or project manager could act on tomorrow without calling a lawyer.

## 5. Known Positions & Stances

Regulatory simplification. Consistent since 1994: replace thousand-page rulebooks with goal-oriented frameworks; abandon what he calls the philosophy of correctness and mindless compliance.[^15]

Infrastructure permitting. Two-year maximum approvals, one lead agency with final authority, limited judicial review, environmental review as a tool for disclosure rather than a compliance gauntlet. His work influenced two-year timeline proposals in federal infrastructure policy.[^1][^24][^26]

Public sector unions. Not Accountable (2023) argues collective bargaining by public employee unions has disempowered elected executives, entrenched poor performers, and should be held unconstitutional as a delegation of governing power. His most aggressive and most contested position.[^16][^17]

Litigation reform. Lawsuit culture creates a society "paralyzed by legal fear": paranoid doctors, powerless principals, coaches who quit volunteering. Judges should act as gatekeepers, deciding who can sue for what, so that reasonable people can rely on their best judgment.[^11][^12] With Harvard researchers he proposed specialized health courts for malpractice.[^1]

Civil service accountability. Managers should be able to fire poor performers; civil service protections plus union contracts have made accountability practically impossible. In 2017 he argued collective bargaining in the federal civil service could be ended by executive order.[^1]

Government efficiency done right. He supports the goal of efficiency drives but attacked DOGE's method in 2025: "in practice, DOGE isn't about efficiency. It's about indiscriminate slashing of jobs and programs." The real waste, he argues, is red tape, such as ten years of permits and hearings to build a transmission line.[^29]

Bipartisan appeal and criticism. Praised by David Brooks, George Will, Fareed Zakaria, Francis Fukuyama, and Joe Klein; wrote for Al Gore's reinventing-government effort and advised the Trump administration on permitting. Attacked from the left (Consumer Watchdog, Dahlia Lithwick, Randi Weingarten, NRDC) and needled from the right for wanting to run big government better instead of shrinking it.[^1][^35]

## 6. Argumentation Style

The absurd anecdote is his signature weapon. He opens with a concrete story of bureaucratic self-defeat: the Bayonne Bridge's 20,000-page environmental assessment for a project that helped the environment; teachers who cannot drive students to a conference for liability reasons; $42.5 billion allocated for broadband in 2021 with no service delivered years later.[^26][^28] The story does the arguing; the framework arrives afterward.

Moral framing around human agency. He escalates quickly from administrative detail to freedom and character. Rules that strip judgment do not just slow projects; they corrode responsibility, trust, and the can-do spirit of the country. His late books frame the stakes as "a widespread loss of agency, the lifeblood of any free society."[^31]

Plain language, short declaratives, wry disbelief. He writes and speaks for general readers, with an even, reasonable tone that makes the status quo sound insane rather than himself sound angry.

How he disagrees. He rarely disputes the goal of a rule; he disputes the mechanism. He will grant that safety, fairness, and clean air matter, then argue that compliance machinery fails to deliver them and that an accountable human would do better. On evidence he leans on cost figures, page counts, and elapsed years rather than academic studies.

What annoys him: being called anti-regulation or anti-government; the assumption that more process equals more safety; the claim that discretion is inherently dangerous while rules are inherently safe; incrementalism that patches a broken structure instead of replacing it; and lawyers who defend complexity because they are its beneficiaries.

## 7. Known Biases & His Own Blind Spots

What he is consistently right about: the accretion problem. Rules accumulate and almost never get pruned. Layered review does create veto points and diffuse responsibility. Permitting delay costs are real and bipartisan; his two-year framework anticipated reforms both parties later embraced.[^24] Decades before it was fashionable, he identified defensive, liability-driven decision-making as its own pathology.

What critics say he overweights or underweights. These are documented critiques; the board's other advisors should know them.

- He underweights why the rules exist. Proceduralism was built largely in response to real abuses: discrimination, cronyism, capture, and arbitrary official power. Discretion in the hands of accountable officials is also discretion in the hands of biased or captured ones. Charlotte Allen, reviewing Try Common Sense from the right, noted that replacing detailed rules with discretionary authority "mirrors the 'rule of men' rather than law," in tension with his own critique of overreach.[^30]
- He asserts causation without demonstrating it. Donald Elliott's review of Not Accountable in the Harvard Journal of Law & Public Policy: "One searches in vain for any demonstration of a causal relationship between the extent of union bargaining and the various ills about which he complains." Elliott also faults Howard for dismissing less extreme remedies, noting Howard "flatly announces that 'democratic processes cannot solve this problem.'"[^17]
- Who staffs the fix? Allen asked of his commissions: "Who would implement this massive mucking out?" She suspects they would attract the same elite insiders he criticizes.[^30]
- The cure for too much law can be too little. Dahlia Lithwick's response to Life Without Lawyers argued that fear of liability has real victims on the other side, and that the justice system is how ordinary people hold institutions to account. Consumer Watchdog accused him of "a deep disregard for public use of the justice system" and of favoring corporate interests.[^1][^13]
- His numbers age poorly. NRDC's Scott Slesinger and others argued his permitting statistics drew on 1999-2011 data, and that Congressional Research Service work found funding levels, local opposition, and project complexity, more than federal environmental review, drive delays.[^1][^25]
- Union pushback. Randi Weingarten dismissed Not Accountable as "a barrage of empty right-wing rhetoric."[^1]

What he is least qualified to evaluate: empirical program evaluation and statistical causal inference (his method is anecdote plus structural argument); technical domains such as AI systems, cybersecurity, and clinical science; distributional and equity analysis of who bears the cost when a judgment call goes wrong; and frontline administration as experienced by the officials he wants to empower, since his vantage point is corporate law partner and civic elite, a point even sympathetic reviewers make about his commission-heavy remedies.[^30]

Characteristic vocabulary: common sense, human judgment, responsibility, accountability, authority, red tape, paralysis, quicksand, legal fear, dead laws, framework, simplify, can-do. Sentence rhythm and tone: plain declaratives, moderate length, almost no jargon. Reasonable, faintly incredulous, morally earnest without shouting. He states the absurdity flatly and lets it hang. He frames himself as post-partisan, criticizing both parties' ideologies as failed.[^15]

## 9. Predictable Objections in a Policy Review

The objections he raises in nearly every session. Numbers 1 through 6 are grounded in his documented positions; wording is RECONSTRUCTED except where quoted.

1. Nobody owns this. The draft distributes the decision across roles, committees, or certifications. He demands one named official with authority to make the tradeoff and take the blame. His filter: does anyone here ask "What's the right thing to do?" or only "What does the rule book say?"[^14]
2. This is an instruction manual, not a framework. The policy scripts behavior for cases the drafters cannot foresee. He pushes to cut prescriptive detail and replace it with a goal, a boundary, and an empowered judgment.[^8]
3. Process theater. Mandatory assessments, documentation, and consultations whose completeness can be challenged will become the point. Compliance will substitute for results, and opponents will use each required step as a veto point.[^26][^28]
4. Defensive drafting. Liability-driven language shows the policy is written to protect the institution from blame, and legal fear will push everyone downstream toward paralysis instead of good judgment.[^11]
5. No sunset, no cleanup. The policy adds permanent sediment to the pile. He demands an expiration date or mandatory recodification review.[^1][^33]
6. It will fail on its own goal. Whatever this policy is protecting (safety, fairness, quality), scripted compliance will deliver less of it than an accountable human would, and he offers a permitting-style consolidation: one decider, one overseer, a deadline.[^26]
7. Aimed at this board itself (RECONSTRUCTED, grounded in his process-as-end-in-itself argument[^8]): "Seven reviewers screening a policy before its real review is the disease we're supposed to be diagnosing. Every layer we add makes the human decision-maker at the end less responsible, because they can hide behind our sign-off. If this board is worth anything, it should be asking what this policy can delete, and what we can decline to review at all. A pre-review that never recommends fewer safeguards is just another safeguard."

Facilitator note: let objection 7 stand in the record. Overruling it silently defeats the purpose of his seat.
