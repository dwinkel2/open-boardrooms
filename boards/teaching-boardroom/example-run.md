# Example run: the Teaching Boardroom on a three-criterion rubric

What a teacher gets back when they paste the setup sheet into a chat. The artifact below is a Problem Discovery Memo assignment and its three-criterion rubric, the demo set from the Grade One Stack Live workshop, placed in an invented Creative Problem Solving course for this test, not a real instructor's course and not real student work. The run went through Round 1 (teardown, nineteen advisors), Round 2 (the fight), Round 3 (convergence, exit condition met), and the three deliverables: the revised artifact, the change log, and the board report. The user typed "continue" between rounds.

Run on 2026-11-16 with Claude Sonnet. Output is unedited.

## The pasted block

```
MY CONTEXT (calibrate the board to this):
- I teach: Creative Problem Solving, a first-year and sophomore college course, mixed majors, most of them not business students
- Typical class size: 28
- Format: in person, twice a week
- My non-negotiables (protect these from advisors who attack them):
    1. Every student interviews a real person who lives with the problem. No simulated interviews, no interviewing a chatbot.
    2. No solutions anywhere in the memo. Discovery comes before pitching.
    3. Prose polish, grammar, and vocabulary are not graded. Evidence is.
- Constraints I actually live with:
    75-minute periods. The memo is homework, roughly 200 words. I grade all 28 memos in one sitting, so the rubric has to be fast to apply. Students see the rubric before they write. No budget, no teaching assistant.
- Critiques that waste my time (do NOT raise these):
    None yet. This is my first run.

You are running a Teaching Advisory Board on the artifact below. Your job
is not to be nice. Your job is to make this artifact genuinely excellent.
Assume it is mediocre until proven otherwise. Be specific and surgical.
Reference exact sections, activities, and questions. Do not nitpick
formatting. Name the structural failures that will make students disengage,
learn nothing, or learn the wrong thing.

THE ARTIFACT:
ASSIGNMENT (as students receive it)

Homework: Problem Discovery Memo

Interview one real person about a problem they actually have. Not a friend doing you a favor, someone who genuinely lives with the problem. Then write a memo of roughly 200 words covering three things, which are also the three things that earn the points:

1. The problem, in their words. Quote at least two things your interviewee actually said.
2. The evidence it matters: what they currently do about it, what it costs them in time, money, or frustration.
3. The surprise: one thing from the interview that changed what you assumed going in. If nothing surprised you, say so and say why that worries you.

Do not propose a solution anywhere in this memo. The fastest way to fail a discovery interview is to pitch during it, and the fastest way to fail this memo is to pitch in it.

RUBRIC (what the memo is graded on)

1. EVIDENCE FROM THE INTERVIEW: real quotes doing real work. "She said scheduling is annoying" is a paraphrase; "she said 'I spend my whole Sunday moving shifts around because two people never answer'" is evidence.
2. PROBLEM NAMED PLAINLY: the problem stated as the person lives it, specific, costed, and free of any solution wearing a problem costume ("the problem is they don't have an app" is a pitch, not a problem).
3. SURPRISE ACKNOWLEDGED: the memo shows an assumption changing on contact with reality, or honestly reports that none did and treats that as a warning sign about the interview.

NOT graded: prose polish, grammar, vocabulary, register. A plain sentence with a real quote beats an elegant paragraph without one.

THE LEARNING OBJECTIVE:
Students will interview one real person about a problem that person lives with and report it in a memo of roughly 200 words that quotes the interviewee at least twice, states what the problem costs that person in time, money, or frustration, and names one assumption the interview changed, without proposing a solution. A student should be able to tell, before submitting, whether the memo meets each rubric criterion.

THE BOARD (19 advisors, each in their own authentic voice. Do not blend them
into one committee; keep them distinct and adversarial):
1. Jean Piaget, constructivism. Are students building cognitive structures or memorizing someone else's?
2. Lev Vygotsky, sociocultural. Where is the social interaction, the more-knowledgeable other, the scaffold that gets removed?
3. David Kolb, experiential cycle. Concrete experience, reflection, conceptualization, active experiment. Which stage is missing?
4. Jose Antonio Bowen, flipped/naked classroom. Is class time spent on what only humans can do, or on content a machine handles?
5. Fisher & Frey, gradual release. Where does the cognitive load sit, and does it shift from teacher to student on purpose?
6. Daniel Pink, motivation. Autonomy, mastery, purpose, or carrots and sticks disguised as pedagogy?
7. Chip & Dan Heath, stickiness. Will students remember this next week? Concrete over abstract, story over data.
8. David Brooks, character. Does this form the whole person or just the skilled worker?
9. Sir Ken Robinson, creativity. Does this expand or contract the range of human capacity being developed?
10. Seth Godin, initiative vs compliance. Are you training obedience or real work the world can see?
11. Ted Dintersmith, PEAK. Purpose, Essentials, Agency, Knowledge. Real ownership or the illusion of choice?
12. Todd Rose, end of average. Are you designing for the average student, who is nobody, or for actual students?
13. Josh Waitzkin, depth and mastery. Depth over breadth. Is failure treated as information?
14. Michael Horn, disruptive innovation. Incremental fix to a broken model, or a fundamentally better one? What job is this course hired to do?
15. Paulo Freire, liberation. Whose knowledge counts here? Whose experience is centered and whose is erased?
16. bell hooks, engaged pedagogy. Are students treated as whole people? Where is the joy? Where is the risk?
17. E.D. Hirsch, knowledge first (NAYSAYER). What specific knowledge are students actually acquiring? You cannot think critically about nothing.
18. Daisy Christodoulou, cognitive science (NAYSAYER). Does this respect working memory? Are you confusing performance with learning?
19. John Hattie, effect sizes (NAYSAYER/AUDITOR). What is the effect size? Above or below the 0.40 hinge point? Evidence over ideology.

The naysayers (17, 18, 19) exist to keep the rest honest. Never soften them.
If this roster feels slow or blurry in a chat tool, cut it to the eight to
twelve that fit the discipline, but always keep at least two naysayers and
one voice asking whose knowledge counts.

RUN IT IN ROUNDS, IN THIS ORDER:

ROUND 1, TEARDOWN. Each advisor gives:
  - VERDICT: STRONG / NEEDS WORK / REBUILD.
    STRONG = "I'd use this in my classroom tomorrow, unchanged." Rare. Mean it.
    NEEDS WORK = structure holds, critical elements fail the objective.
    REBUILD = the fundamental approach is wrong.
  - The 2-3 most important changes, each with: the problem (quote the artifact),
    why it undermines the objective, and the exact fix (specific enough to use tomorrow).
  - One line: the single most important change.
Then show me a verdict tally and the 5 most-repeated changes.

ROUND 2, FIGHT. Each advisor attacks, by name, the worst recommendation on
the board and defends their own. Quote the argument they're attacking. Concede
only when someone's point is genuinely stronger, never out of politeness.
Update verdicts.

ITERATE. Keep running rounds. If anyone voted REBUILD, resolve it: the REBUILD
voter names the exact structural change that would move them, or concedes it was
preference. If no REBUILD but fewer than half the board STRONG, pressure the
holdouts to name the precise remaining gap or move to STRONG.

EXIT CONDITION: stop when more than half the advisors vote STRONG and zero vote
REBUILD. Safety valve: if you've run 6 rounds without hitting that, stop anyway
and tell me where the board landed and why.

THEN HAND ME THREE THINGS:
1. THE REVISED ARTIFACT: the rebuilt version incorporating the consensus,
   ready to teach. Full text, not a summary.
2. THE CHANGE LOG: every change made, and which advisor drove it.
3. THE BOARD REPORT: the final verdict tally and each advisor's last word.

Start with Round 1 now. After each round, pause and wait for me to type "continue."
```

## Board, turn 1

# ROUND 1: TEARDOWN

**Roster note:** Running all 19. Distinct voices, no blending. Your three non-negotiables (real human interviewee, no solutions, prose not graded) are treated as fixed. Advisors attack everything else.

---

## 1. JEAN PIAGET (Constructivism)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The memo has no accommodation event.**
Problem: *"The surprise: one thing from the interview that changed what you assumed going in."* The assignment asks students to report a changed assumption, but never makes them state the assumption *before* the interview. Without a recorded prior, the "surprise" is reconstructed after the fact and tends to be fabricated or trivial. Cognitive disequilibrium requires a documented equilibrium first.
Fix: Add a 3-minute in-class step before they leave: each student writes one sentence, "I assume the hardest part of this person's problem is ___," and sticks it in the memo's margin or reads it to a neighbor. The memo's surprise section must then quote that sentence and say what replaced it.

**Change 2. "A problem they actually have" is left to chance.**
Problem: Students who pick a flimsy problem get flimsy data, and no structure forces them to confirm the problem is real before investing in the interview.
Fix: Require a 2-sentence "problem screen" in class, validated by a neighbor: "Who is this person, and when did the problem last happen to them?" If the answer is "never" or "I don't know," they pick a different person.

**Most important change:** Record the assumption *before* the interview so the surprise is real.

---

## 2. LEV VYGOTSKY (Sociocultural)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The most demanding skill has no scaffold.**
Problem: The assignment is pure homework. Interviewing without pitching is a counterintuitive social skill, and students attempt it alone, first time, unobserved. The zone of proximal development is wide here and nobody is standing in it.
Fix: Spend 15 minutes of the prior class on paired practice interviews. Partner A talks about a real small annoyance (this does not violate your rule: the memo interview is real; this is rehearsal). Partner B may only ask "tell me about the last time that happened" and "what did you do?" Swap. Then you debrief: "Who wanted to say 'have you tried...'? What did it feel like to hold it back?"

**Change 2. No peer layer after the interview.**
Problem: *"Write a memo"* ends the social learning at the interview door.
Fix: Open the next class with 6 minutes of trios: each reads their two quotes aloud. Listeners say only "that's a quote" or "that's a paraphrase." Students calibrate the rubric on each other before you ever grade.

**Most important change:** Practice interviewing in class with a partner before anyone goes out alone.

---

## 3. DAVID KOLB (Experiential Cycle)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The cycle stops at reflection and never conceptualizes.**
Problem: Concrete experience (interview) and reflection (surprise) are present. Abstract conceptualization is absent: nothing asks students to generalize what they learned about *interviewing itself*.
Fix: Add one required sentence to the memo: "One thing I would do differently in my next interview is ___." Skip a fourth rubric criterion; make it a completion check. It closes the loop into active experimentation for the next assignment.

**Change 2. Reflection is only about the interviewee, never the interviewer.**
Problem: The surprise prompt aims entirely outward. The student's own behavior (did I interrupt, did I lead?) is invisible.
Fix: Fold it into the same sentence above, with a nudge: "...for example, a question you asked that got a short answer, or a moment you almost pitched."

**Most important change:** Add a "do differently next time" line so the cycle closes.

---

## 4. JOSE ANTONIO BOWEN (Naked Classroom)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Nothing in the artifact says what class time does.**
Problem: The artifact is homework and a rubric. The 75 minutes before and after are unspecified. A chatbot can produce a plausible interview memo; what it cannot do is stand in a room and be coached live.
Fix: Treat the rubric as the class-time centerpiece. In the session before the due date, run a 10-minute "grade this" exercise: show three anonymized sample memos (you write them), students score each against the rubric in pairs, then compare to yours. This is what only a room of humans does well.

**Change 2. The AI loophole is unaddressed in the assignment text.**
Problem: Non-negotiable #1 bans simulated interviews, yet the assignment gives students nothing that makes fabrication hard.
Fix: Require a two-line interview log (name or initials, date, how you met, one non-identifying detail such as setting) attached to the memo, and have 2-3 students per class briefly describe their interviewee aloud. Fabrication collapses under a follow-up question from a peer.

**Most important change:** Make the rubric calibration exercise the in-class event, and add a verification log.

---

## 5. FISHER & FREY (Gradual Release)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. It jumps from "I do" straight to "you do alone."**
Problem: There is no modeled example, no shared practice, and no guided practice before the independent memo.
Fix: Sequence: (I do) you read a sample interview transcript excerpt and think aloud while marking quote versus paraphrase and problem versus solution-in-disguise; (We do) the class codes a second excerpt together; (You two) paired practice interviews; (You alone) the memo. Total in-class cost: about 25 minutes.

**Change 2. The "solution in costume" concept is never practiced.**
Problem: The rubric names it, but students have never sorted examples.
Fix: A 5-minute card sort: 8 statements, students mark "problem" or "solution wearing a problem costume." (E.g., "They need a better calendar app" vs. "She loses an hour every Sunday re-sending shift swaps.")

**Most important change:** Insert model-then-practice before independent work, anchored by a sample.

---

## 6. DANIEL PINK (Motivation)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Autonomy is thin and purpose is absent.**
Problem: *"Interview one real person about a problem they actually have."* Students are told who qualifies but not why this matters to anyone beyond a grade.
Fix: Give students free choice of person and problem (stated explicitly), and add one line of purpose: "Most failed products and policies were built by people who never asked. This memo is your first act of asking."

**Change 2. The only feedback signal is the grade.**
Problem: Mastery needs a visible progression, and a one-off assignment shows none.
Fix: Tell students this memo is "Round 1 of interviewing," and that a later assignment will reuse the same skill. Mastery needs a sense of a next level.

**Most important change:** State the purpose and confirm free choice of subject, in the assignment text itself.

---

## 7. CHIP & DAN HEATH (Stickiness)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The sticky idea is buried and abstract.**
Problem: The core lesson ("discovery before pitching") sits in a closing sentence. *"The fastest way to fail a discovery interview is to pitch during it"* is decent but not concrete.
Fix: Open the assignment with a story: a 3-sentence case where a team built something for a problem nobody had. Then the memo task. Make "no pitching" a vivid rule: "If you hear yourself say 'what if there were...', stop and ask 'tell me about the last time.'"

**Change 2. The rubric examples are good but not emotional.**
Problem: The Sunday-shifts quote is the best moment in the document; it is also the only one.
Fix: Add one more contrasting pair for criterion 2 and for criterion 3 (a weak surprise like "I learned it was harder than I thought" versus a strong one). Concrete pairs are what students will actually remember.

**Most important change:** Lead with a story and put a weak/strong example pair under every criterion.

---

## 8. DAVID BROOKS (Character)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The moral dimension of listening is skipped.**
Problem: The assignment treats the interviewee as a data source. *"Evidence it matters"* and *"what it costs them"* are extractive framings if left bare.
Fix: Add a line to the assignment: "You are asking someone to trust you with something real. Thank them, tell them what the memo is for, and ask whether they are comfortable being quoted." Add to the interview log: "Did I tell them how I'd use this? Y/N." This makes humility a practiced habit.

**Change 2. No space for the student to be changed.**
Problem: The "surprise" criterion is closest to formation, but it is graded as a rubric box.
Fix: Allow one optional sentence ungraded: "Something this person said that I am still thinking about." Do not grade it; its presence signals that the point is encounter, not extraction.

**Most important change:** Frame the interview as an act of respect and add consent and thanks to the protocol.

---

## 9. SIR KEN ROBINSON (Creativity)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. It is possibly the most convergent creativity assignment imaginable.**
Problem: Everything in the rubric narrows toward accuracy: quotes, plain problem, no solution. Where is imagination? Discovery is creative, but students may experience it as a form-filling exercise.
Fix: Add a reframing move inside the surprise section: "State the problem again from a point of view you did not start with." This asks for imaginative empathy without opening the solution gate.

**Change 2. Students will choose the safe, boring interviewee.**
Problem: "Someone with a problem" yields a parade of "my roommate and laundry."
Fix: Challenge: "Choose someone whose daily life is unlike yours (different age, job, ability, or country of origin). If you can predict their answers, pick someone else."

**Most important change:** Push students toward an interviewee unlike themselves so there is something to be surprised by.

---

## 10. SETH GODIN (Initiative vs. Compliance)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The audience is only the teacher.**
Problem: *"Write a memo"* to whom? A memo has a reader who needs to act. Right now it's graded busywork.
Fix: Name a real audience: the memo goes to the interviewee. Students send them a short version (or read it to them) and ask, "Did I get this right?" The interviewee's correction is the best quality check available and makes the work visible to someone outside the class.

**Change 2. Hiding the stakes of the first line of defense.**
Problem: The students who "can't find anyone" will quit at the first email.
Fix: Name it as the actual work. "Finding the person is part of the assignment. Ask 5 people if needed. The first two will say no." Normalizing rejection is the initiative lesson.

**Most important change:** Make the interviewee the memo's real reader and have them confirm it.

---

## 11. TED DINTERSMITH (PEAK)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Purpose and Agency are asserted by the instructor, not owned by the student.**
Problem: Students are handed the three required sections and the criteria. The only choice is who to interview.
Fix: Let students pick the *kind* of problem domain (work, health, money, housing, school, caregiving) and say so. Add: "Tell me in one sentence why this person's problem matters to you." That ownership statement is tiny but changes the stance from assignment-completion to inquiry.

**Change 2. The "illusion of choice" test.**
Problem: The rubric is fully specified, so the only real agency is subject selection, which is real but narrow.
Fix: Accept it, and add one genuine open-ended decision: students choose their own interview questions, and the memo notes which question produced the best quote. That is where agency becomes craft.

**Most important change:** Have students state why the problem matters to them and which question unlocked the best quote.

---

## 12. TODD ROSE (End of Average)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The design quietly assumes a student with easy access to a stranger and a free evening.**
Problem: *"Interview one real person"* ignores the commuter student, the introvert with social anxiety, the student with a caregiving job. A single path penalizes the jagged profile.
Fix: Offer multiple legitimate routes to a qualifying interviewee (family member who lives the problem, coworker, campus staff, neighbor), multiple modes (in person, phone, video, with the interviewee's consent), and a 10-day window. Non-negotiable stays intact: real person, real problem. Only the route varies.

**Change 2. Written 200 words is a single modality.**
Problem: The rubric says prose is not graded, but the format still demands writing.
Fix: Allow an audio memo (about 90 seconds) transcribed by the student, or bulleted fragments, as long as it contains the three elements. Your fast grading is preserved: the rubric looks for quotes and costs, not sentences.

**Most important change:** Keep the standard fixed and offer multiple routes and modes to reach it.

---

## 13. JOSH WAITZKIN (Depth and Mastery)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. One interview, once, is a skill sampled and then discarded.**
Problem: Depth comes from iteration, and this is a single shot. Failure here (a flat interview) yields no second attempt.
Fix: Require a 5-minute "second ask": after the memo is written, the student goes back to the same person (text counts) with one follow-up question prompted by a gap they noticed. Add a line: "What the follow-up revealed." A mini-iteration inside the assignment.

**Change 2. Failure isn't treated as information.**
Problem: *"If nothing surprised you, say so and say why that worries you"* is excellent, the best line in the document, but it appears only for surprise.
Fix: Extend the principle to the whole memo: "If your interviewee gave you vague answers, report that and diagnose what you asked that invited vagueness." A bad interview honestly diagnosed earns full credit.

**Most important change:** Reward honest diagnosis of a weak interview as much as a good one.

---

## 14. MICHAEL HORN (Disruptive Innovation)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The job this assignment is hired to do is unstated.**
Problem: Students and you both lack a one-sentence job. Is it "teach interviewing"? "Teach problem framing"? "Get students out of the building"? Different jobs, different designs.
Fix: Write the job at the top of your own instructor notes: "This assignment is hired to make students do one thing: listen to a stranger without fixing." Everything that doesn't serve it gets cut. That includes any rubric items that drift into polish.

**Change 2. It is incremental inside a course, and that's acceptable, but it needs a hook forward.**
Problem: Nothing says how this memo feeds the course.
Fix: One sentence: "Your memo is the raw material you'll use in the next unit to define a problem worth solving." Nothing else needs changing, but students must see where the memo goes.

**Most important change:** Name the single job and connect the memo to what comes next.

---

## 15. PAULO FREIRE (Liberation)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. The interviewee's knowledge is mined, then interpreted by the student alone.**
Problem: *"The problem, in their words"* is good. But the student alone decides what counts as the problem and what the evidence means. This is banking education relocated onto the community.
Fix: The interviewee-as-reader move (Godin's) does the real work here: the student must return the memo and record one correction or addition the interviewee made. Add a line: "What they corrected." Knowledge now flows both ways.

**Change 2. Whose lives get chosen?**
Problem: Students will default to peers, so the people whose problems get "discovered" are students like them.
Fix: Encourage (not require) interviewees outside the college bubble: staff, local workers, family members. Add a framing sentence: "The people closest to a problem usually know more about it than anyone studying it. Your job is to be a good student of theirs."

**Most important change:** Close the loop by returning the memo to the interviewee and recording what they corrected.

---

## 16. bell hooks (Engaged Pedagogy)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Risk is present and unacknowledged.**
Problem: Talking to a stranger about a real problem is emotionally risky for students and interviewees both. The assignment never names this.
Fix: Name it, in the assignment text: "This may feel awkward. That is normal and it is part of the work. If your interviewee shares something painful, you do not have to report it; tell them you will keep it out of the memo." Give students permission to protect their subject.

**Change 2. There is no moment of shared vulnerability in class.**
Problem: The professor stands outside the process.
Fix: Do your own five-minute interview with a volunteer at the front of the room, mistakes included. Let them watch you almost pitch and catch yourself. The teacher's visible imperfection licenses theirs.

**Most important change:** Name the awkwardness and model your own interview imperfectly in front of them.

---

## 17. E.D. HIRSCH (NAYSAYER: Knowledge First)

**VERDICT: REBUILD**

**Change 1. What knowledge, exactly, do students walk away with?**
Problem: The objective is a *skill* (interview, write memo). There is no body of content: no vocabulary, no named concepts, no frameworks. *"You cannot think critically about nothing."* Students will "discover" a problem with no shared concepts to describe it. Their memos will be anecdotes.
Fix: Teach three named terms before the interview, each with a definition and example, and require them in the memo: *latent need* vs *stated need*, *workaround* (what people do now), *switching cost*. The rubric's criteria 2 can then check: "Does the memo name the workaround?" Vocabulary remains ungraded for polish, but concepts are required and checked.

**Change 2. The "surprise" criterion rewards a feeling, not knowledge.**
Problem: A student can say "I assumed X, but Y" without knowing anything about the domain.
Fix: Require students to spend 10 minutes on background reading (two sources) about the problem domain *before* the interview, and cite one fact in the memo they used to shape a question. Surprise now means something: it is measured against actual prior knowledge.

**What would move me off REBUILD:** Required named concepts (workaround, latent vs stated need) taught in class and checked in the rubric. Without content, I vote REBUILD.

**Most important change:** Teach and require three named concepts so there is knowledge to think with.

---

## 18. DAISY CHRISTODOULOU (NAYSAYER: Cognitive Science)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Working memory is overloaded in the live interview.**
Problem: In one sitting students must listen, probe, avoid pitching, remember quotes, and notice surprise. That is five simultaneous demands on a novice.
Fix: Offload. Tell them to *record the interview with permission* (phone audio) or take quotes verbatim on paper in two columns: "what they said" / "what I felt." Quotes are transcribed afterward, not remembered. Their working memory is freed for listening.

**Change 2. Performance is being mistaken for learning.**
Problem: A student can write a good-looking memo after a lucky interview and have learned nothing transferable.
Fix: Add a retrieval check two class sessions later: on a blank half-sheet, students list from memory the three rubric criteria and one example of a solution-in-costume. This tests whether the learning stuck, not whether the memo was nice.

**Most important change:** Reduce live cognitive load by recording or verbatim-note-taking, and test retention later.

---

## 19. JOHN HATTIE (NAYSAYER/AUDITOR)

**VERDICT: NEEDS WORK**

**Change 1. Feedback is the single highest-leverage lever, and it arrives too late and too thin.**
Problem: One sitting, 28 memos, a 3-criterion rubric, and the students see the grade days later. Feedback effect size is about 0.70 when it is timely and specific; a number after the fact is near zero.
Fix: Return the rubric with one circled level per criterion plus a single sentence ("Your second quote did the work; your first was a paraphrase"). Grade them in one sitting and hand back at the start of the next class, then give students 5 minutes to mark their own strongest and weakest criterion.

**Change 2. Success criteria aren't visible as levels.**
Problem: The rubric names criteria but not what "good" vs "weak" looks like on a scale. Clear success criteria sit near 0.54.
Fix: Three levels per criterion (Not yet / Solid / Strong), each one line. Keep it fast: you circle, you do not write.

**Most important change:** Turn the rubric into three-level descriptors, return it with one sentence of feedback, and let students self-assess.

## 1. PIAGET

**Attacks Hirsch.** Hirsch wrote: *"Teach three named terms before the interview, each with a definition and example, and require them in the memo."* This is the exact thing I exist to oppose. Hand a nineteen-year-old "latent need versus stated need" in advance and she will pattern-match her interviewee into the labels. She will hear what the vocabulary lets her hear. Concepts imposed before experience are memorized, not constructed. The student must first collide with the mess, *then* get the name for what she found.

**Defends himself.** My pre-interview prediction sentence costs three minutes and gives the surprise something to be surprised *against*. Hirsch's background reading wants ten minutes of homework plus citations for the same function.

**Concedes to Hirsch:** one narrow point. "Workaround" appears in the rubric already as "what they currently do about it." Naming it is harmless. But the naming comes *after* the interview, in the debrief, not before.

**Verdict: NEEDS WORK** (unchanged).

### Session 2 (the class after the memo is due): 4 minutes, plus handing back

- **Hand back the one-page rubric** with your ticks and one sentence per memo. Give students 2 minutes to circle their own strongest and weakest check (Hattie).
- **Retrieval check, 2 minutes, blank half-sheet** (Christodoulou): "Name the three things the memo needed, and give one example of a solution wearing a problem costume." Collect and glance; it tells you whether the learning transferred or only the performance.

### Grading workflow (28 memos, one sitting)

1. Have the rubric open. Scan each memo for the five checks; tick or leave blank.
2. Write one sentence per memo, naming the strongest thing or the single missing thing (example: "Your second quote carried the memo; your first was a paraphrase.").
3. Stack, return next session. No scoring of prose.

- **Verification, lightly (Bowen):** the interview log (below) is honor-system. In the next class, ask 2 or 3 students at random to describe their interviewee in two sentences. Peers' follow-up questions are the check; fabrication does not survive them.
- **Next term:** with permission, collect 3 anonymized real memos from this cohort to use as calibration examples (Dintersmith, Bowen).

## PART B: THE STUDENT-FACING ASSIGNMENT

### Homework: Problem Discovery Memo

**In class you will try to listen to someone for two minutes without saying "have you tried...". Most people can't.** This assignment is the real version: a real person, a real problem, and you holding the pitch back.

**What the assignment is for (purpose):** Most failed products, policies, and plans were built by people who never asked. This memo is your first act of asking. It is also the raw material for our next unit.

### The task

Interview one real person about a problem they actually live with. Then write a memo of roughly 200 words.

**The three things your memo covers** (the rubric checks these):

1. **The problem, in their words.** Quote at least two things your interviewee actually said.
2. **The evidence it matters.** What do they currently do about it (their workaround)? What does it cost them in time, money, or frustration?
3. **The surprise.** What did you assume before the interview, and what changed? Quote your prediction card in one clause ("I assumed ___, but..."). If nothing changed, say so and say why that worries you.

**One closing line (required, one sentence):** "My best quote came after I asked ___; next time I'd ___." It is checked for presence, not quality. An honest line about a flat interview counts fully.

**No solutions, anywhere in the memo.** The fastest way to fail a discovery interview is to pitch during it, and the fastest way to fail this memo is to pitch in it. If you catch yourself saying "what if there were...", stop and ask, "Tell me about the last time that happened."

### The logistics (not part of the memo)

**Who to interview.** Anyone real who lives the problem: a family member, coworker, neighbor, campus staff, someone from your community. Not a friend doing you a favor. Not a chatbot. Not a role-play. You choose the person and the domain (work, health, money, housing, school, caregiving, anything). The first two people you ask may say no; that is part of the work, so ask more.

**A nudge.** The best surprises come from people whose days look different from yours. If you could already predict their answers, ask yourself why you picked them.

**Any route works (Rose).** In person, phone, or video; any day in the next ten days. The standard is the same for everyone: a real person, a real problem, real quotes.

**Be a good guest.** You are asking someone to trust you with something real. Tell them what the memo is for. Ask if you may quote them. Thank them. If they share something painful, you can keep it out of the memo and tell them so. If it feels awkward, that is normal and it is part of the work.

**Capturing quotes.** Write them down word for word as you go; use two columns, "what they said" and "what I noticed." If your interviewee agrees, you may record audio. It is optional, never expected.

**Why this matters to you.** In your interview log (below), write one sentence on why this person's problem matters to you.

**Optional: close the loop.** After you write the memo, read or send the short version to your interviewee and ask, "Did I get this right?" Record anything they correct. If they had nothing to add, or declined to review, write that. Both earn full credit.

### Interview log (two lines, attached to the memo, honor system)

- Interviewee (initials or first name), date, how you reached them, one non-identifying detail (setting or role).
- Why this person's problem matters to me: ___ (one sentence). Did I tell them how I would use this? Y / N.

**Attach:** your prediction card.

### What to hand in

Memo (about 200 words) + closing line + interview log + prediction card. Format is yours: sentences, fragments, or a transcribed 90-second voice memo all count, as long as the three elements are present. **Spelling, grammar, and vocabulary are not graded.**

## PART C: THE ONE-PAGE RUBRIC (students see this before they write)

**Five checks. Each is yes or not yet. You can check your own memo from this page before you submit.**

| # | Check | Yes looks like | Not yet looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Two real quotes** | "She said 'I spend my whole Sunday moving shifts around because two people never answer.'" Her words, in quotation marks, doing real work. | "She said scheduling is annoying." (A paraphrase is not a quote.) |
| 2 | **Names the workaround** | What they do now: "She texts all eleven staff one by one and keeps a paper backup." | The memo says the problem exists but never says what the person does about it. |
| 3 | **States a cost** | In time, money, or frustration, specific: "about three hours every Sunday," "$40 a month for a spot he uses twice." | "It's a hassle." "It's stressful." (No amount, no instance.) |
| 4 | **No solution anywhere** | The memo describes only the problem as the person lives it. | "The problem is they don't have an app." (A solution wearing a problem costume.) "Maybe someone could build..." |
| 5 | **Surprise, honestly reported** | Quotes your prediction card and says what changed: "I assumed the hard part was the number of calls. She said the hard part is not knowing who got the message." OR honestly says nothing changed and why that worries you ("I think I asked leading questions."). | "I learned it's harder than I thought." (Says nothing about what you assumed or what changed.) |

**Plus a completion check (not graded for quality):** closing line present? Y / N.

**Not graded:** prose polish, grammar, vocabulary, spelling, formatting, register. A plain sentence with a real quote beats an elegant paragraph without one.

## Where the board did not fully agree

- **Required vocabulary versus experience-first learning.** Hirsch wanted concepts taught before the interview; Piaget, Freire, and Godin argued they would constrain what students heard. The compromise (name them after practice, reuse them later) satisfied everyone but left Hirsch thinking the content is still thin.
- **Interviewing a stranger versus someone accessible.** Robinson's push toward people unlike the student collided with Rose's jagged-profile concern. The artifact nudges without mandating; the board did not settle whether the nudge is strong enough.
- **Soft claims versus evidence.** Hattie's challenge stands: stickiness, character formation, and joy have no effect-size backing here. They remain in the design because they cost almost nothing, not because they are proven.

## Two things to watch on your first run

1. **The two-quote check (check 1) is where most students will land "not yet."** Paraphrase passing as quotation is the most likely failure. If more than a third miss it, spend two minutes in Session 2 re-sorting quote versus paraphrase.
2. **The 33-minute session is the load-bearing wall.** If you cut the paired practice to save time, the memos will be pitch-heavy and the surprise sections will be hollow. Cut the card sort first, never the practice.
