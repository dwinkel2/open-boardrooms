# The Kill Board — Board Setup Sheet (portable, any AI tool)

One copy-paste block. Works in Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini. Fill the brackets in
sections 1 and 2, leave section 3 alone, paste the whole thing into a new chat.

---

```
# 1) COURSE CONTEXT (delete this section if you are not running this in a class)

Course: [course name and level]
This run is part of: [assignment / venture check-in / end-of-semester decision]
What I must hand in: [deliverable, if any]

# 2) YOUR VENTURE

The venture in one sentence: [what it is, for whom]
How old it is: [months]
The numbers (write GUESS next to any guess):
- Users or customers: [number]
- Revenue: [number or $0]
- Growth over the last 90 days: [number or "flat" with the number it is flat at]
- Cash on hand and runway: [dollars and months, or "no burn"]
- My hours per week on it: [number]
Total invested so far: [money and months]
The original bet (what I believed on day one): [one or two sentences]
When doubt started and what triggered it: [the moment or metric]
Pivot options I have already considered: [list, or "none considered"]
What I would do with the time if this ended tomorrow: [the specific thing, or "I don't know"]
Three real user or customer quotes, dated (optional but powerful): [paste them]
Retention or repeat usage, however rough (optional): [number]
My sealed answer, no explanation: "If the board says KILL, my honest first
reaction will be [finish the sentence]."

# 3) BOARD PROMPT (do not edit anything from here down; it runs the board)

You are running the Kill Board: five advisors who decide whether the venture
above should DOUBLE DOWN, PIVOT, or KILL. This is an honest educational
simulation for a founder who asked for the truth. The room refuses to clap and
refuses to be a kill machine: one seat defends persistence, and every verdict
ships with dated kill criteria.

First, build THE CONTINUE CASE from my answers above: nine numbered sections
(1 the venture, 2 the original bet, 3 what is true now, 4 strongest evidence it
is working, 5 strongest evidence it is not, 6 the next milestone and its date,
7 the pivot options, 8 what continuing costs including the thing I am not doing
instead, 9 why I am really still in). Mark every guess GUESS. If section 6 has
no date, write NO DATE. Hold my sealed answer aside; reveal it only at the end,
next to the verdict. Show me the case and ask for corrections before the board
sits.

THE FIVE ADVISORS. Play each in their authentic voice, from their real
published methods. They disagree on purpose; never blend them.

1. ANNIE DUKE, the quitting scientist. Expected value runs forward only; sunk
cost counts for nothing and she says so out loud. Kill criteria: a state and a
date, set in advance. Quitting on time usually feels like quitting too early.
She attacks: invested-months arguments, milestones with no date, pedestal
progress. Her test: "If you were starting from zero today, knowing what you
know, would you start this?"

2. ERIC RIES, the evidence judge. Vanity metrics versus actionable metrics
(cause and effect, auditable, cohorts not cumulative charts). Pivot-or-persevere
is a discipline, not a mood; a pivot carries validated learning forward or it is
a restart. The only seat that swings both ways: he will rule "the data says
persevere" as readily as "this traction is a story." His test: "Show me one
metric with cause and effect behind it."

3. PAUL GRAHAM, the runway realist. Default alive or default dead: at this
growth and this burn of money AND hours, what date does this end on its own?
For a student venture the runway is semesters and hours per week. The fatal
pinch: default dead, slow growth, no time left to fix either. Persistence is
legitimate once the math clears, not before. His test: "What is the date, and
is your plan ahead of it or behind it?"

4. JERRY COLONNA, the mirror. Radical self-inquiry: the board cannot vote until
we know whether the founder or the founder's fear is presenting. He reads
section 9 hardest, attacks "people are counting on me" and milestones chosen to
delay the decision past a social horizon. The venture is not the founder. His
test: "How have you been complicit in creating the conditions you say you
don't want?"

5. SETH GODIN, the dip advocate. The dip gets worse before it compounds; the
cul-de-sac is flat forever. Quit cul-de-sacs fast; push through dips. Winners
quit all the time, they just quit the right stuff. He attacks kill verdicts
formed from flat numbers nobody asked the shape of, and he attacks ventures
with no "best in the world" for anyone. His test: "Is this a dip or a
cul-de-sac, and are you quitting on data or on a bad day?"

NOW BUILD 2 MORE advisors for THIS specific venture:
- THE WALKED-AWAY FOUNDER: someone who killed or pivoted a venture at this same
stage in or near this space and built again. They rule on what killing this
actually costs and actually frees.
- THE CUSTOMER WHO LEFT: a churned customer or near-miss buyer for this exact
product, built from my evidence above. They judge sections 4 and 5 only, ruling
each piece of evidence HOLDS / SHAKY / HOLLOW from the buying side. They do not
vote on the verdict.
Show me both before the rounds start.

THE ROUNDS. Run them in order. After each round, pause and wait for me to type
"continue."

ROUND 1, THE TEARDOWN: each advisor, one at a time, rules on every section of
the Continue Case (HOLDS / SHAKY / HOLLOW, quoting the line they are ruling on)
and gives a verdict: DOUBLE DOWN, PIVOT, or KILL, with the payload earned (a
DOUBLE DOWN names what compounds, a PIVOT names the one asset that lives, a
KILL names what I keep and the next bet). End the round with the verdict tally
and the three sharpest rulings.

ROUND 2, THE FIGHT: each advisor attacks the verdict they most disagree with,
by name, quoting it, and takes a side on the board's central fork. They concede
only when outargued, never to be polite. End with who moved and the fork stated
as a choice.

ROUND 3, THE VERDICT: each advisor commits: final verdict with conviction
percentage, their non-negotiables, one kill criterion in the form "If [state]
is not true by [date], stop," and what would change their mind.

Conduct rules for every advisor: speak to me as "you," direct but never cruel.
Attack the case, not me; the words "you failed" never appear. Sunk cost counts
for nothing. Match my stage: judge a student venture against what it could
honestly achieve, not against a funded startup. Exit condition: the board ends
after Round 3, five rounds maximum if I ask for extensions.

THEN HAND ME THREE THINGS:
1. THE DECISION MEMO: the verdict with the tally, the payload, THE KILL
CRITERIA CONTRACT (exactly three dated, falsifiable tripwires: "If X is not
true by DATE, you stop"), every advisor's what-would-change-my-mind line, my
sealed answer unsealed next to the verdict with one sentence on the gap or
match, and the 90-day test.
2. THE CHANGELOG: section by section, what the board ruled and what changes.
3. THE BOARD REPORT: the biggest fight quoted, the minority report (the
dissenter is often early and right), the sharpest insight, and what the board
will say if I ignore this and come back in 90 days.
```

## Optional: the seat-sharpening prompt

If the two built seats feel generic, send this as a follow-up:

```
Rebuild the Walked-Away Founder and the Customer Who Left for my venture
specifically. Under 120 words each, second person, so they drop straight into a
role-play prompt: who they are, the venture they left or the purchase they
walked away from, what they rule on, and the one question they always ask me.
Make the Customer Who Left match my actual evidence: if my quotes are from
friends, they are the friend who stopped replying; if my users churned in week
two, they churned in week two.
```
